# ROBOTICS.md: Part 8 of the DFx course, robotic systems

How to use: this file sits at the repo root next to `CLAUDE.md`. Every rule in `CLAUDE.md` still applies: verified sources only, no invented case studies, no employer content, metric units, the module page structure, the verification tools, and the token discipline. This file adds four modules and the rules specific to them. Kick it off with the prompt at the bottom.

## Why

The course covers DFx by process. It does not cover the product most of its readers are increasingly asked to build: a robot. Robots concentrate every DFx problem in one place, an actuator: precision gears, bearings with preload, a motor, an encoder, a brake, seals and grease, a harness, a controller, all inside a housing whose bore alignment decides whether the assembly lasts a year or a month. Then the company has to build hundreds of them, test each one, and certify the machine they go into. Part 8 teaches that, with the same standard of evidence as the rest of the course.

## Rules specific to Part 8

1. **No target company.** The modules are written for "a company building mobile or manipulating robots at fleet scale". Never name, describe, or imply the internal practices of any specific private company as a design target. Public companies and products may be used as examples only when the fact is public and cited: a published patent, a datasheet, a standard, a paper, a court filing, a published teardown, a company's own published talk or blog. Never write "companies like X need".
2. **Nothing from the author's interviews or conversations with companies.** Same rule as employer content in `CLAUDE.md`. Leave `<!-- AUTHOR: -->` placeholders where a personal note might go.
3. **Named products stay factual and cited.** A statement like "strain wave gears lose torsional stiffness under repeated overload" is fine with a source. A statement about a named manufacturer's field failure rate is not, unless it is in a published document you read.
4. **A mechanism enters the course only from a public source.** Every mechanism described in Part 8 is traceable to a patent, a catalog, a standard, a paper, or a published talk listed in `SOURCES.md`. A mechanism the author describes in conversation is used only if he also supplies the public source for it; otherwise teach the public family it belongs to and leave the specific configuration out.
5. **Numbers are ranges from sources, not design values.** Backlash classes, gear accuracy grades, bearing L10 formulas, seal drag torque, tread hardness, and similar figures come from standards, catalogs, and papers with citations. Where sources vary, give the range and say so.

Rotary reducer literature (for the short upstream section only): Harmonic Drive and Nabtesco published catalogs and engineering data (ratcheting, lost motion, rated versus momentary peak torque, life curves); Wittenstein and Neugart planetary catalogs (backlash classes, efficiency, run-in); Wensing et al. 2017 on the MIT Cheetah proprioceptive actuator; Grimminger et al. 2020, Open Dynamic Robot Initiative; Seok et al. on the Cheetah leg; Katz's MIT Mini Cheetah actuator thesis; published moteus and ODrive documentation on encoder calibration and cogging; Tesla's published Optimus talks for actuator architecture (only what was said publicly); NASA Lessons Learned and NTRS reports on actuator, gear, and bearing failures on flight hardware; published Mars rover actuator and drill anomaly reports; Kiva Systems, AutoStore, Ocado, Exotec, and Attabotics patents and public product data for lattice and grid storage robots (drive units, wheel change mechanisms, lifting, charging); the Ocado versus AutoStore litigation record as a public description of grid robot mechanics; published teardowns of collaborative robot joints; SKF, Schaeffler, and NSK published bearing failure atlases and mounting guides; Klüber and similar published grease selection guides; polyurethane wheel manufacturers' published tread data.

Manufacturing and test: published gearbox end-of-line test literature (back-to-back or four-square rigs, torque ripple, transmission error, no-load torque signatures, acoustic testing); published press-fit force-displacement monitoring literature; encoder alignment and calibration papers; HALT and HASS literature (Hobbs); MIL-STD-810 for vibration and shock methods; published MES and traceability practice in automotive; time study literature for MODAPTS and MOST (Zandin, *MOST Work Measurement Systems*); line balancing and takt time (any standard IE text, cited); discrete event simulation of assembly lines (published FlexSim case studies or academic papers).

## The four modules

Numbering continues after the capstone; Module 16 stays the capstone of the process modules and the index page gets a "Part 8: Robotic systems" heading. Two modules cover mechanisms and they are deliberately different families: Module 17 is rotary joints (arms, manipulators, surgical and collaborative robots), Module 18 is linear drives that move a robot along a structure (mobile, climbing, and gantry robots). Do not merge them and do not let one family's architecture table leak into the other.

### 17. Rotary actuators and gear trains for robot joints: DFx and failure modes

Learning objectives around: choosing between planetary, strain wave, cycloidal, direct or quasi-direct drive, and cable or tendon drive for a given torque, speed, backlash, stiffness, transparency, and cost target; reading an actuator specification (rated, peak, momentary peak torque; backlash and lost motion; torsional stiffness; efficiency; L10); the anatomy of a robot joint as a manufacturing problem.

Architectures for the design rules table: planetary (one to three stages), strain wave, cycloidal, quasi-direct drive, and cable or tendon drive with capstans (the dominant transmission in low-volume surgical and teleoperated manipulators, where the motor is remote and the joint is driven through pretensioned cables; public sources include patents, the Raven surgical robot papers, and the da Vinci Research Kit documentation).

Core content: the actuator as a stack of tolerances (housing bore concentricity and parallelism, bearing fits and preload, gear center distance, encoder air gap) with a worked stack-up; gear failure modes per AGMA 1010 (pitting, micropitting, scuffing, wear, tooth root fatigue, and the misalignment edge loading that causes most of them early); bearing failure modes per ISO 15243 (brinelling from shock and transport, false brinelling from vibration, spalling, preload loss, contamination); strain wave specifics (flexspline fatigue, ratcheting under overload, wave generator bearing wear, lost motion growth over life); cycloidal and planetary specifics (pin and roller wear, carrier misalignment, load sharing); cable drive specifics (cable construction and fatigue over small pulleys, termination methods, pretension setting and loss, pulley alignment and fleet angle, creep and stretch, why cable life is the service interval); lubrication (fill quantity, leakage paths, grease degradation, temperature, and cleanliness requirements where the joint is near a sterile or clean boundary); motor and electronics failures relevant to the mechanical engineer (winding overtemperature, magnet demagnetization, encoder contamination, cable fatigue at moving joints, connector wear); brakes (wear, glazing, holding torque drift); fasteners under vibration (Junker test, thread locker, torque control); frames and welds (fatigue at brackets).

Manufacturing challenges as a second design rules table: gear grade versus process (hobbed, shaved, ground, powder metal, molded) and cost; bore machining datum strategy (link to Module 3 and the GD&T course); bearing fit selection for rotating versus stationary ring and thermal growth; press-fit process control; preload setting methods; grease dosing; run-in; encoder mounting; magnet handling; cable termination and pretensioning as controlled processes with measured results; cleanliness; traceability of every serialized part; what changes at low volume (tens to hundreds of units a year: fixtures over automation, first article and inspection depth, configuration control, and the cost of a design change when every unit is serialized and installed).

Real-world examples: at least three verified (candidates: a NASA flight actuator anomaly report; a published rover drive or drill failure; the MIT Cheetah or ODRI actuator papers on why quasi-direct drive was chosen over high-ratio gearing; the Raven or dVRK papers on cable-driven joint design and its failure modes; a published teardown of a collaborative robot joint).

Exercise: pick an actuator for a stated joint (torque, speed, duty cycle, backlash limit, volume, cost) from three candidate architectures and defend it; compute L10 for the output bearing under a stated load spectrum; do the bore-to-bore stack-up; size a cable for a stated joint torque, pulley diameter, and cycle life target.

### 18. Drive units for mobile and climbing robots: linear drives, their failure modes, and their manufacturing

**Scope.** This module is about drives that move a robot along a structure, not about rotary reducers for arm joints (those are Module 17). The job the mechanism does is rotary-to-linear conversion: a motor turns a pinion, a roller pinion, a roller worm or drum, a screw, or a belt pulley, and contact with a rack, screw nut, or belt turns that rotation into travel along a track or rack. Harmonic and cycloidal reducers appear only as the gearbox that may sit between motor and pinion, in one short section that points back to Module 17. If this module's architecture table compares strain wave to cycloidal, it is off scope.

Learning objectives around: choosing a linear drive for a stated load, travel, speed, orientation (horizontal versus vertical climbing), positioning accuracy, backlash, life, and cost; reading a linear drive specification (rated and peak linear force, backlash or preload state, positional accuracy and repeatability over travel, efficiency, back-drivability and self-locking, permitted misalignment, life in km of travel or cycles); understanding a drive unit and its rack as one tolerance chain across the whole structure.

Architectures for the design rules table (each row: how it converts motion, contact type, backlash and how it is removed, efficiency, load and travel it suits, climbing suitability including back-driving and brake needs, dominant failure modes, manufacturing implications, source):

- Involute rack and pinion (hardened and ground racks, helical versus straight, preloaded split pinions and dual-pinion electrical preload)
- Roller pinion on a rack: bearing-supported rollers on the pinion engaging a rack whose tooth form is generated for rolling contact; near-zero backlash by roller preload against opposing flanks; the published roller pinion system literature is the primary source
- Roller worm or roller drum on rack tracks: discrete roller assemblies on a rotating worm or drum engaging tracks on a rack, the same rolling-contact family in a different geometry; teach it from public patents and catalogs only, unnamed as to any specific robot product
- Ball screw and planetary roller screw (for lifts and short heavy travel)
- Lead screw (self-locking, low efficiency, why it is chosen for holding loads)
- Belt and chain drives (long travel, low cost, elasticity and tensioning)
- Friction wheel and traction drives on rails (slip, contamination, tread wear)

Core content: rolling versus sliding contact and what it means for efficiency, wear, lubrication, and noise; Hertzian contact stress on rollers and rack flanks as the sizing limit; backlash removal by opposing rollers or preloaded pinions and what preload does to friction and life; the tolerance chain from motor to rack: pinion or roller assembly runout, roller axis parallelism to the rack tooth, center distance between drive axis and rack pitch line, rack straightness and pitch accuracy within a segment, and pitch and alignment error at every joint between rack segments over a long or tall structure (the joint is where accuracy is lost, with a worked example of accumulated pitch error over a stated height); climbing specifics: the drive holds the robot's weight, so back-driving, brake sizing and holding-torque drift, redundant brakes, fall arrest and overspeed devices as used on rack-and-pinion hoists, and what the standards for those hoists require; failure modes: roller bearing wear and seizure, roller flat spotting under sustained static load, rack flank wear, pitting and micropitting under high contact stress, fretting at rack joints, contamination and debris on open racks, lubricant starvation on lightly lubricated rolling drives, preload loss, pinion or roller assembly fastener loosening (Junker test), misalignment-driven edge loading of rollers, and the rotary reducer and motor failures upstream (in one section, with gear failure nomenclature per AGMA 1010 and bearing damage per ISO 15243); wheels and tread, brakes, cable fatigue, connectors, batteries and charging contacts as wear items, in a short section.

Manufacturing challenges as a second design rules table: rack manufacturing (hardening and grinding, pitch accuracy class per DIN 3962 or ISO 1328, straightness, hardness and case depth, joining segments with pitch gauges and alignment fixtures); roller assembly manufacturing (roller bearing selection and fit, roller runout, needle versus ball rollers, hardness and surface finish, preload setting and measurement); drive unit housing (bore parallelism and position relative to the rail interface, datum strategy, link to Module 3 and the GD&T course); rack installation on the structure as a manufacturing process in its own right (the structure's straightness and squareness become the drive's accuracy; fixtures, laser alignment, acceptance checks); press fits with force-displacement monitoring; grease dosing; run-in; traceability of every serialized drive unit and rack segment.

Real-world examples: at least three verified (candidates: the roller pinion system white paper and catalog data on backlash, efficiency, and life; roller pinion and roller-on-rack patents; the rack-and-pinion hoist and mast climbing work platform standards and any published incident report involving rack drive failure; grid storage robot patents and the Ocado versus AutoStore litigation record for drive and wheel mechanisms; a NASA or rover actuator anomaly for the upstream reducer section only).

Exercise: select a linear drive for a stated climbing robot (mass, payload, travel, speed, acceleration, duty cycle, positioning requirement, cost) from three candidate architectures and defend it; size the roller contact stress and the brake; compute accumulated pitch error over a stated number of rack segments with a stated joint tolerance and state whether the positioning requirement is met; do the drive-axis-to-rack tolerance stack.

### 19. Test stations, fixtures, and jigs for actuators and robots

Learning objectives around: designing the test strategy for an actuator and for a whole robot through EVT, DVT, PVT and production; specifying a test station; designing the alignment fixtures that make the assembly correct in the first place.

Core content: the test ladder (incoming inspection, sub-assembly, actuator end-of-line, robot end-of-line, burn-in, sample audit); actuator end-of-line tests and what each detects: no-load current and torque versus speed sweep (friction, stiction, seal drag, assembly damage), breakaway torque, torque ripple and cogging, backlash and lost motion by torsional hysteresis curve, torsional stiffness, efficiency under load on a dynamometer or back-to-back rig, and for linear drive units the same signatures over travel (force versus position, backlash at the rack, roller runout, breakaway force), cable pretension verification on cable-driven joints, brake holding torque, thermal rise, acoustic signature, encoder calibration and offset, leak or IP test; friction taught properly (Stribeck behavior, stiction, seal and grease contribution, temperature dependence, why the run-in curve matters, what a friction signature limit should be based on); robot end-of-line (drive, lift, climb or reach, sensor and camera calibration on a target fixture, communications, battery and charging, safety stop and brake, a short mission on a test track or a sample structure); burn-in and HASS; golden units and gauge R&R on every test station (link to the Six Sigma course); pass and fail limits from data, not guesses; test data captured per serial number.

Fixtures and jigs: axis parallelism and concentricity fixtures (dial indicator pairs, precision mandrels, laser alignment, CMM when it is worth it); bearing press fixtures with force-displacement monitoring and what the curve shows; gear mesh contact pattern checks; preload setting fixtures; wheel alignment and toe on a drive module; frame flatness and rail straightness checks; assembly fixtures that locate on the datum features from the drawing (link to the GD&T course); fixture design rules (3-2-1 location, repeatability, poka-yoke, ergonomics for heavy parts, calibration and control of the fixture itself as a gauge).

Real-world examples: at least three verified (candidates: published gearbox EOL test methods; a published encoder calibration procedure; ISO 9283 test methods as used in a published robot datasheet; a NASA or automotive published press-fit monitoring case).

Exercise: write the end-of-line test specification for a stated drive unit (tests, limits with rationale, cycle time, fixture list); design the parallelism check for a two-bore housing and state its uncertainty.

### 20. Building the fleet: system-level DFx, safety certification, and the assembly line

Learning objectives around: what changes when a robot goes from ten units to a thousand; the certifications that shape the design early; designing the assembly line and its time budget.

Core content: system-level DFx (harness and cable management in moving joints, connector selection and mating cycles, battery pack assembly and its safety requirements, sensor mounting and calibration provisions, serviceability of wear items in the field, modularity of drive units); the safety and compliance map for mobile and industrial robots (ISO 3691-4, ANSI/RIA R15.08, UL 3100, ISO 10218, ISO/TS 15066, ISO 13849 performance levels, EMC, IP ratings) and what each demands from the mechanical design before DVT; traceability (serial numbers on every actuator, test data per unit, MES, ECO discipline at fleet scale, link to Module 14); serviceability, cleaning, and sterilization-driven design constraints for robots used in clinical or food environments, in one short section with public standards cited; field failure feedback (RMA analysis, failure mode Pareto, closing the loop to design); the assembly line: process flow, sub-assembly versus main line, takt time from demand, line balancing, time studies with MODAPTS and MOST and when each is appropriate, discrete event simulation (what a FlexSim style model answers and what it does not), ergonomics and lifting for heavy modules, kitting, poka-yoke, capacity planning; the economic case (cost per unit versus volume, tooling and fixture investment, test station capital, when to automate a station).

Real-world examples: at least three verified (candidates: a published AMR or AGV safety certification write-up; a published time study or line balancing case; a published account of a robotics company's ramp with numbers from the company itself).

Exercise: from a stated demand and a stated process list with times, compute takt, balance the line, identify the bottleneck station, and state the fixture and test station count; then a MODAPTS estimate for one assembly step.
