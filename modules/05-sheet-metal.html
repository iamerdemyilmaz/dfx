Module 5

DFM for sheet metal

Sheet metal is a flat pattern that gets folded. Every rule in this module comes from what happens to a flat sheet when a punch pushes it into a die: it stretches on the outside, compresses on the inside, wants to spring back, and pulls any nearby hole out of round. Design the flat pattern with those facts in mind and the folded part takes care of itself.

Learning objectives

  • Specify an inside bend radius appropriate to the material and thickness, and a minimum flange length the tooling can form.
  • Calculate bend allowance and developed flat length from thickness, inside radius, bend angle, and K-factor.
  • Place holes, slots, and countersinks far enough from bends and edges that forming does not distort them.
  • Design bend reliefs, hems, tabs, and notches to standard proportions.
  • Specify self-clinching hardware with the hole, edge distance, sheet hardness, and installation side it needs.
  • State realistic tolerances for features across one, two, and three bends, and account for coating thickness.

Why this matters

On 4 November 2016 the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission announced a recall of about 2.8 million top-load washing machines. The hazard statement is a sentence about a sheet-metal joint: "The washing machine top can detach unexpectedly from the washing machine chassis during use, posing a risk of injury from impact."[7] The manufacturer had received 733 reports of excessive vibration or of the top detaching, including nine injuries. The remedy offered to owners was a free in-home repair that reinforced the top.[7]

The public record does not give the joint design, and this course does not speculate about it. What the record does show is that a sheet-metal attachment on a mass-produced appliance, subjected to a dynamic load the design had to survive (an unbalanced drum at high spin speed), could separate, and that the fix was applied to 2.8 million units in customers' homes. Sheet-metal joints are cheap to make and easy to underestimate. A tab, a flange, a self-clinching nut, or a spot weld carries the load only if the geometry around it was designed for the load, and the geometry around it is set by the rules in this module.

You will more often meet the small versions: a hole that went oval because it sat too close to a bend, a flange too short for the press brake to grip, a PEM nut that bulged the panel edge, a bracket whose three-bend tolerance stack meant the mounting holes did not line up. Each one is a flat-pattern decision.

How the process shapes the rules

A sheet-metal part is made in two stages. First the flat pattern is cut from a sheet by laser, punch press, or waterjet, including every hole, slot, and relief. Then the flat is bent, usually on a press brake, where a punch presses the sheet into a V-shaped die to form each bend in turn. Stamping dies do both in one tool at high volume, but the geometry rules are the same. Four consequences follow.

  1. The material stretches and compresses. On the outside of a bend the metal is in tension; on the inside it is in compression. Somewhere between lies a neutral axis whose length does not change, and its position is what the K-factor describes.[3],[4] Everything near the bend moves: a hole close to the bend line stretches into an oval.
  2. The die needs something to hold. A flange shorter than the die opening cannot be formed; it falls into the die.
  3. The bend has a minimum radius. Below it, the outside fibres crack, especially across the grain and in less ductile alloys.
  4. Each bend adds error. Angle, radius, and material springback vary slightly every time, so a dimension across three bends is far looser than one across a single flat.

Bend radius versus thickness

The inside bend radius should be at least equal to the material thickness to prevent fracture or distortion, and consistent radii throughout a part make it cheaper to form because the brake keeps one tool set.[2] One fabricator's standard tooling forms inside radii of 0.76, 1.52, 2.29, and 3.05 mm (0.030, 0.060, 0.090, and 0.120 in) in sheet from 0.61 to 6.35 mm thick.[1] Alloy matters as much as thickness: 5052 aluminium is the common sheet grade because of its ductility, while 6061, popular for machining, can be brittle and crack when bent.[2] Very short flanges and tight radii in thick material are the combination that fractures.[2]

Two habits: specify the inside radius, not the outside, because the inside radius is what the punch sets, and use the same radius on every bend of a part unless a function demands otherwise.

K-factor, bend allowance, and the flat pattern

To cut the right flat, you need to know how much material is consumed in each bend. The neutral axis, the line in the thickness that neither stretches nor compresses, sits at a distance t from the inside surface. The K-factor is that distance as a fraction of the sheet thickness T: K = t / T, and in practice it lies between 0 and 1.[3] The bend allowance (BA) is the arc length of the neutral axis through the bend:[3]

BA = α · (π / 180) · (Ri + K · T)

where α is the bend angle in degrees, Ri the inside radius, and T the thickness. The developed flat length is the sum of the flat lengths (measured to the tangent points of the bends) plus the bend allowance of each bend.[3]

The K-factor is not a constant. It depends on material, thickness, hardness, bend radius, and bending method, and is determined experimentally by bending test strips and measuring them.[3] For aluminium it typically lies between 0.30 and 0.50, with most air-bending jobs falling between 0.33 and 0.45.[4] Many CAD systems default to about 0.44 to 0.45, a value long associated with mild steel in reference tables.[9] The design consequence is that your CAD flat pattern is an estimate. Fabricators develop their own K-factors or bend deductions for their tooling, and for a tight part you should either ask for theirs or send the folded-part drawing and let them develop the flat.

Shops often work with bend deduction (BD) rather than allowance: the amount to subtract from the sum of the outside flange lengths. The two are related through the outside setback: BA = 2 · setback − BD, where for bends up to 90° the setback is tan(α/2) · (Ri + T).[3] Allowance and deduction are often confused on shop-floor charts, so state which one you mean.

Minimum flange length

A flange must be long enough for the press-brake die to support it while the punch forms the bend. The published minimum from one fabricator is four times the material thickness, measured from the inside of the bend to the edge of the flange.[1] Shorter flanges need special narrow dies or are simply not formable, and thick material makes the problem worse.[2] If a design needs a very short return, consider a hem or a separate part.

Holes, slots, and their distance from bends and edges

Material near a bend moves during forming. A hole inside that zone stretches into an oval, and a hole near a free edge can bulge the edge when a fastener is installed. The guides give the clearances as multiples of material thickness (MT), and they differ slightly, which tells you they are practical minimums rather than physics.

If a hole must sit closer to a bend than the rule allows, the usual fix is to cut a relief slot on the bend line beside the hole, so the bend zone does not pass through it, or to pierce the hole after forming.

Bend relief, hems, tabs, and notches

Bend relief. Where a bend ends inside the part (a flange narrower than the sheet), the material at the end of the bend tears unless a small relief notch is cut beside it in the flat. Common practice is a relief at least as wide as the material thickness and deep enough to reach past the bend zone; your fabricator's flat-pattern software will add a standard relief if you do not, so ask what it is.

Hems. A hem folds the edge back on itself for stiffness and a safe edge. Protolabs gives a minimum inside diameter equal to the material thickness and a hem return length of six times the thickness.[1] Closed hems in thick or hard material crack; an open (teardrop) hem is the safer choice there.

Notches and tabs. Minimum notch width is the material thickness or 1.02 mm, whichever is greater, and notch length should not exceed five times the width; minimum tab width is twice the thickness or 3.2 mm, whichever is greater.[1] Long thin tabs bend during handling and long thin notches are slow to cut.

Countersinks. Standard angles are 82°, 90°, 100°, and 120°, with major diameters from 2.29 to 12.7 mm and a diameter tolerance of +0.51/−0.25 mm.[1] A countersink in thin sheet leaves very little material; check that the sheet is thick enough for the depth you need.

Offsets. Where a flange must step by a small amount, standard offset tools exist in a fixed set of heights (one fabricator lists 0.76 to 7.9 mm) with a tolerance of about ±0.30 mm; design to a standard height rather than an arbitrary one.[1]

Tolerances across bends

Each bend contributes angle, radius, and springback variation. Protolabs quotes ±0.13 mm for a single surface in sheet under 3.3 mm, ±0.38 to 0.64 mm for dimensions across two bends, and ±0.76 to 1.02 mm across three or more, with bend angles at ±1°.[1] Xometry's standard tolerances are ±0.38 mm edge-to-bend or bend-to-feature, ±0.76 mm edge-to-edge over multiple formed surfaces, and ±0.25 mm for features on the same flat surface.[2] ISO 2768-1 general tolerances apply to sheet-metal formed parts as well as machined ones,[8] and a fabricator will often quote against class m or c.

The design rule that follows: locate mating features on the same flat, or across one bend at most. A hole pattern split across three bends will not line up with the mating part, and no amount of tolerance tightening will fix it as cheaply as moving the holes.

Self-clinching hardware

Sheet metal is too thin to tap, so threads are added as hardware: self-clinching nuts, studs, and standoffs pressed into punched holes, weld nuts, or rivet nuts. Self-clinching fasteners are the most common in enclosures, and the manufacturer's handbook gives the rules.[6]

  • Sheet thickness. Self-clinching fasteners can provide strong threads in metal as thin as 0.3 mm, with the minimum depending on the fastener type and size.[6] Each fastener is specified for a sheet thickness range; check it.
  • Hole. The mounting hole is punched or drilled to the catalogue size (not the thread size), held to +0.08/−0 mm, and must not be chamfered or have broken edges over 0.127 mm.[6]
  • Installation side. For sheets 2.29 mm and thicker, install from the punch side, because the die side of a punched hole has a larger break-out diameter.[6]
  • Sheet hardness. The sheet must be softer than the fastener so the metal flows into the fastener's undercut. The handbook's maxima: aluminium fasteners into sheet under HRB 50, unhardened steel under HRB 60, 300-series stainless under HRB 70, hardened steel under HRB 80, hardened stainless under HRB 88. Typical sheets: 5052 aluminium HRB 15 to 30, cold-rolled steel HRB 40 to 75, 6061-T6 HRB 50 to 55, annealed 304 stainless HRB 80 and above.[6] That last line is the one that catches people: a standard steel nut will not clinch reliably in 304 stainless.
  • Centreline to edge. Each fastener has a published minimum distance from hole centreline to sheet edge. When it is violated, "visible bulging of the edge of the panel may occur", which indicates the clinch is not fully formed and reduces performance.[5] The published value applies to one edge only; a fastener near two edges, or near a bend (measure to the outside of the bend radius), needs more room or a supporting anvil during installation.[6]

Sequence the operations deliberately. Hardware is usually installed after forming, so its hole must remain reachable by the press; and it is usually installed before painting or after plating, depending on whether the thread may be coated. Say which in the drawing notes.

Coatings and hardware interaction

Powder coat and paint add thickness. A common powder-coat specification calls for a film of 60 to 80 µm,[10] which on both sides of a clearance hole reduces its diameter by up to 0.16 mm, and on a mating face changes a stack-up. Mask threads and datum surfaces, or specify hardware installation after coating with the hole sized to allow it. Plating is thinner but must be considered on press-fit and self-clinching features. Module 13 covers coating selection; the sheet-metal rule is simply to decide the coating and the hardware sequence before the flat pattern is released.

Real-world example: a washing machine top

The CPSC recall notice of 4 November 2016 covers about 2.8 million top-load washers manufactured between March 2011 and November 2016. The hazard: "The washing machine top can detach unexpectedly from the washing machine chassis during use, posing a risk of injury from impact." The manufacturer had 733 reports of excessive vibration or the top detaching, with nine injuries including a broken jaw. Owners were offered a free in-home repair that reinforced the top, a rebate toward a new machine, or a refund for recent purchases.[7]

The design detail is not public, so the lesson stays general and is still worth drawing. A sheet-metal top attached to a sheet-metal chassis is a joint made of thin material, fasteners or tabs, and the geometry around them: edge distances, flange lengths, hem stiffness, the number of attachment points, and whether the load path runs through a flat or through a bend. Under a steady load such joints are rarely the weak point. Under the dynamic load of an unbalanced drum at spin speed, every one of those geometric choices is tested millions of times, and the fastener that sits too close to an edge, the flange that is too short to grip, or the tab that carries load across a bend becomes the place the joint gives way. The remedy, "reinforcing the washer's top", is what a redesigned sheet-metal joint looks like when it has to be applied after the fact, one home at a time.

The DFM habit this teaches is to treat every sheet-metal attachment as a load path and to check it against the rules in Table 2 with the dynamic load in mind, not the static one, before the tooling is cut.

Common mistakes

  1. Holes too close to a bend. Consequence: oval holes and fasteners that do not fit. Fix: 2.5 to 4 × MT from the bend line, a relief slot on the bend line beside the hole, or pierce after forming.
  2. Flanges shorter than four times the thickness. Consequence: the die cannot hold the flange; the shop asks for a redesign or special tooling. Fix: lengthen the flange, use a hem, or split the part.
  3. Inside radius smaller than the thickness, or a different radius on every bend. Consequence: cracked outer fibres, especially in 6061 or across the grain; extra tool changes. Fix: R ≥ MT, one radius per part, ductile alloy.
  4. Trusting the CAD K-factor for a tight part. Consequence: the developed length is off by tenths of a millimetre per bend and the folded part misses its tolerance. Fix: ask the fabricator for their K-factor or bend deduction, or send the folded drawing and let them develop the flat.
  5. Mating hole patterns across several bends. Consequence: ±0.76 to 1.02 mm variation where the mating part allows ±0.25 mm. Fix: put mating features on one flat or across one bend.
  6. Self-clinching nuts in the wrong sheet. Consequence: a steel nut that spins in annealed 304 stainless, or bulges the panel edge because it sits too close to it. Fix: match fastener hardness to sheet hardness per the handbook, hold the hole tolerance, respect the centreline-to-edge minimum.
  7. Forgetting coating thickness. Consequence: clearance holes 0.16 mm smaller after powder coat; threads full of paint. Fix: size holes for the coated condition, mask threads, and state the hardware-and-coating sequence.
  8. Missing bend reliefs. Consequence: tearing at the ends of partial flanges, or a relief added by the shop where you did not expect one. Fix: draw reliefs at least MT wide reaching past the bend zone, or confirm the shop's standard relief.

Worked exercise: flat pattern of a U-bracket

This is an illustrative example with invented but realistic numbers, not a real program.

A U-shaped bracket is formed from 1.5 mm 5052 aluminium with two 90° bends and an inside bend radius of 1.5 mm. The outside dimensions are: base 60 mm between the outer faces of the two legs, and legs 30 mm high from the outer face of the base to the leg edge. An M4 clearance hole of 4.5 mm diameter sits in one leg with its edge 5 mm from the bend, and an M4 self-clinching nut is planned in the base 6 mm from the base's free edge.

Tasks. (a) Check the flange length and bend radius against the rules. (b) Compute the bend allowance per bend with K = 0.40 and the developed flat length. (c) Recompute with K = 0.33 and state the difference. (d) Check the hole-to-bend distance and note what must be checked for the self-clinching nut.

Show the worked solution

(a) Flange and radius. Minimum flange 4 × MT = 6 mm; the 30 mm legs are fine. Inside radius 1.5 mm equals MT, meeting the R ≥ MT rule, and 5052 is the ductile grade. No change.

(b) Bend allowance and flat length, K = 0.40. Each bend: BA = 90 × (π/180) × (1.5 + 0.40 × 1.5) = 1.5708 × 2.10 = 3.30 mm. Flat lengths are measured to the tangent points. The outside of each leg to the outside of the base is 30 mm; subtracting the outside radius (Ri + T = 3.0 mm) gives a leg flat of 27.0 mm. The base outside length is 60 mm; subtracting two outside radii gives a base flat of 54.0 mm. Developed length = 27.0 + 54.0 + 27.0 + 2 × 3.30 = 114.6 mm.

(c) K = 0.33. BA = 1.5708 × (1.5 + 0.33 × 1.5) = 1.5708 × 1.995 = 3.13 mm. Developed length = 108.0 + 2 × 3.13 = 114.3 mm. The difference is 0.33 mm over two bends, about 0.17 mm per bend. On a bracket with ±0.76 mm tolerance across bends it does not matter; on a part whose leg height is toleranced ±0.25 mm it consumes most of the tolerance before the brake has varied at all, which is why the fabricator's K-factor should be used for tight parts.

(d) Hole and hardware. Hole to bend: 5 mm ≥ 2.5 × 1.5 = 3.75 mm (Xometry) and ≥ 4 × 1.5 = 6 mm (Protolabs' distortion warning) is not met by 1 mm. Either move the hole to 6 mm from the bend or accept a small risk of ovality and confirm with the fabricator. For the self-clinching nut: look up the catalogue mounting-hole diameter for an M4 nut in 1.5 mm sheet (it is not 4 mm), hold it to +0.08/−0 mm without chamfer, confirm 5052 at HRB 15 to 30 is soft enough for a steel nut (it is), and check that 6 mm from the edge meets the published minimum centreline-to-edge distance for that part number; if it does not, expect edge bulging and move the nut inboard.

Key takeaways

  • Design the flat pattern, not the folded part. Every clearance, relief, and hem is decided before the first bend.
  • Inside radius at least the thickness, one radius per part, and a ductile alloy such as 5052 for anything that bends.
  • Bend allowance is the arc of the neutral axis, BA = α·(π/180)·(Ri + K·T). The K-factor is experimental; use the fabricator's for tight parts.
  • Flanges at least 4 × thickness; holes 2.5 to 4 × thickness from bends and 2 × thickness from edges; slots and countersinks further still.
  • Tolerances loosen with every bend, from about ±0.13 mm on a flat to about ±1 mm across three bends. Put mating features on one flat.
  • Self-clinching hardware needs the catalogue hole at +0.08/−0 mm, a sheet softer than the fastener, the punch side in thick sheet, and the published edge distance.
  • Coating adds up to 0.16 mm to a hole's effective closure. Decide coating and hardware sequence before release.
