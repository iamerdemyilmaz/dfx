Module 9

DFM for PCB and PCBA

A printed circuit board (PCB) is made by one supplier and populated into a printed circuit board assembly (PCBA) by another, and both have design rules that the schematic never shows. This module covers the rules that decide whether a board can be fabricated, assembled, and inspected at yield, and the component decisions that decide whether it can still be built in three years.

Learning objectives

  • Name the IPC standards that govern board design, land patterns, DFX practice, and assembly acceptability, and state what each one is for.
  • Size a trace for current with the IPC-2221 relationship and check it against a fabricator's minimum trace and space.
  • Specify drills, annular rings, via types, aspect ratios, and solder mask features that a board house can build at yield.
  • Lay out a panel with rails, fiducials, and tooling holes, and orient components for reflow and wave soldering.
  • Apply thermal relief where it prevents soldering defects and omit it where current matters more.
  • Assess component lifecycle and material risk, including the tin-whisker lesson, before a part is designed in.

Before you start

3. Copper near a V-cut (scored) panel edge should be kept back about

Why this matters

On 19 May 1998 the Galaxy IV communications satellite failed in orbit, taking down most of the pagers in the United States. It was one of three Hughes HS601 satellites that lost a side of their redundant spacecraft control processors that year. In August 1998 Hughes reported publicly that "electrical shorts involving tin-plated relay switches are the most likely cause", and that its investigators had "narrowed down the most probable cause to a tin-plated latching relay that serves as an on/off switch"; under certain conditions "a tiny, crystalline structure, less than the width of a human hair, can grow and bridge a relay terminal to its case, causing an electrical short".[14] The structure is a tin whisker. NASA's list of documented whisker failures records Galaxy IV as a complete loss and includes, among others, a 1986 heart pacemaker recall, missile programmes, and relay failures at several nuclear power plants.[14],[15]

Nothing on the Galaxy IV schematic was wrong. A relay was specified, a relay was fitted, and the relay worked. The failure came from a material choice inside a purchased component, pure tin plating, that the circuit designer may never have seen. That is the shape of PCBA DFM: the board and the components are made by other people to rules you do not write, and your job is to design so that their rules, their processes, and their materials produce the board you drew. This module gives you the rules for the board, the assembly, and the components, in that order.

The IPC standards you will meet

IPC is the trade association that publishes the standards the electronics industry designs and inspects to. Four of them matter for DFM.

  • IPC-2221B, Generic Standard on Printed Board Design (2012). The foundation design standard for the 2220 series, covering the generic requirements for printed board design whether single-sided, double-sided, or multilayer: materials, conductor spacing, current capacity, holes, and mechanical requirements.[1] Its trace-current relationship is the one every calculator uses (below).
  • IPC-7351B, Generic Requirements for Surface Mount Design and Land Pattern Standard. Defines land patterns (footprints) for surface-mount components and the courtyard, a boundary around the part body, leads, and lands that lets two components be placed adjacent without violating the clearances of IPC-2221.[4],[16]
  • IPC-2231, DFX Guidelines (2019; current revision IPC-2231A). A guideline, not a specification, that establishes "best practice methodology for use in developing a formal design for excellence (DFX) process for laying out printed board assemblies".[3] It is the electronics industry's version of this course.
  • IPC-A-610H, Acceptability of Electronic Assemblies (September 2020). The visual acceptance criteria for assembled boards, used by every contract manufacturer and inspector; revision H removed the former "target condition" category and added guidance for bottom-terminated components.[2] It defines the three product classes (Class 1 general electronic, Class 2 dedicated service, Class 3 high performance) that set how tight the acceptance criteria are, and your fabricator's capability table is usually written against those classes.

You will not read the standards cover to cover. You need to know which one your contract manufacturer inspects to (usually IPC-A-610 Class 2 unless you say otherwise), and that when a fabricator's DFM report cites a rule, it is usually traceable to IPC-2221 or to their own process capability, and you should know which.

Trace width and spacing

Two limits govern a trace. The fabricator's minimum is a process capability: one high-volume board house lists 0.10 mm trace and 0.10 mm space (4/4 mil) for one- and two-layer boards and 0.09 mm (3.5 mil) for multilayer at 1 oz copper; a prototype and high-density house quotes 3 mil trace and space as an example capability, with 8 mil spacing minimum on 2 oz copper.[5],[6] Finer is possible at tighter process tiers and higher cost. The design rule that follows: use the fabricator's standard minimum plus margin everywhere, and their fine-line capability only where the pitch forces it.

The other limit is current. IPC-2221 relates current I to the trace cross-section A (in square mils) and the allowable temperature rise ΔT in °C:[7]

I = k · ΔT0.44 · A0.725, with k = 0.048 for external layers and 0.024 for internal layers.

Solve for A, then divide by the copper thickness (1 oz copper is about 35 µm, or 1.37 mil) to get the width. For 1 A at a 10 °C rise on a 1 oz external layer the published tables give a trace of about 0.25 mm (10 mil).[7] Internal traces are allowed half the current for the same width because they cannot lose heat to the air, which is why power should run on outer layers or on heavier copper. The same source notes that IPC-2152 (2009) is the newer, test-based standard for current capacity and gives more accurate results for modern boards than the IPC-2221 relationship, which dates from military work of the 1950s and 1960s.[7] The IPC-2221 formula is conservative for external traces and remains the common quick check; use IPC-2152 when the answer matters.

Spacing for voltage is tabulated in IPC-2221 by voltage range, layer (internal, external bare, external coated), and altitude. Read the table rather than guessing; for low-voltage logic the fabricator's minimum spacing governs, and for mains or high-voltage sections IPC-2221's spacing governs and is far larger.

Drills, annular rings, and vias

A plated through-hole is a drilled hole, a copper pad, and the ring of copper between them. Drilling wanders and layers misregister, so the pad must be larger than the hole by enough to leave a ring after the worst-case offset. One board house requires a plated-hole annular ring of at least 0.20 mm and recommends 0.25 mm at 1 oz copper, a minimum drill of 0.15 mm, and a minimum via pad of 0.25 mm around a 0.15 mm via hole; non-plated holes need a larger keep-out of about 0.45 mm.[6] Another expresses the same rule as a formula: finished hole-to-copper clearance equals drill-to-copper clearance plus twice the plating thickness, so a 6 mil drill clearance with 1 mil plating gives an 8 mil (0.20 mm) finished clearance.[5]

Depth matters too. The aspect ratio, board thickness divided by hole diameter, limits how well plating reaches the middle of the hole. Sierra Circuits quotes 1:10 for through-holes on a 1.57 mm (62 mil) board and 0.75:1 for microvias.[5] On a 1.6 mm board that means a 0.16 mm drill is the limit for a reliable through-via, and standard FR-4 thicknesses run 0.4 to 2.0 mm with 1.6 mm the default.[6]

Via types, in order of cost: through vias pass through the whole board and are drilled mechanically; blind vias connect an outer layer to an inner layer; buried vias connect inner layers only; microvias are laser-drilled, small, and shallow, and are used in high-density interconnect (HDI) boards. Every step beyond through vias adds lamination cycles and cost. A via placed in a surface-mount pad (via-in-pad) must be filled and plated over or the solder wicks into it and starves the joint. Keep vias out of pads unless the density leaves no choice, and then specify filled and capped vias explicitly.

Solder mask and silkscreen

Solder mask keeps solder where it belongs, and it has its own registration tolerance and minimum feature. Sierra Circuits' standard tier gives a minimum mask dam (the strip of mask between two pads) of 0.13 mm (5 mil), a mask feature tolerance of about 0.04 mm, and a registration of about 0.05 mm; the high-volume house lists a 0.10 mm minimum mask bridge for standard colours at 1 oz copper.[5],[6] When pads are closer than the dam minimum the fabricator gangs the mask opening, and solder can bridge between them. Fine-pitch parts therefore push you toward the fabricator's better mask tier or toward a larger pitch. Silkscreen needs clearance too: Sierra lists 0.11 mm to mask, 0.15 mm to copper, and 0.20 mm to holes and routed edges (4.5, 6, and 8 mil).[5] Silkscreen on a pad is ink in a solder joint; the fabricator will clip it, and your reference designator disappears.

Land patterns, courtyards, and spacing

IPC-7351B defines the land pattern for each component family and the courtyard around it, so that two courtyards placed edge to edge respect the electrical clearances of IPC-2221.[4],[16] Use the standard's patterns or the manufacturer's recommended footprint; do not shrink pads to make room, because pad size sets the solder volume and the self-alignment of the part during reflow. Around the courtyard, leave room for three other things: the pick-and-place nozzle and the rework tool (a hot-air nozzle around a BGA needs several millimetres), the test probe (Module 11: a probe needs 0.7 mm beside components under 2 mm tall and 1.91 mm beside components over 4 mm), and the inspection camera's line of sight.[13] Keep copper 0.2 mm from routed board edges and 0.4 mm from V-cut edges, and keep a 3 mm clear strip along edges that ride on conveyor rails.[6],[13]

Reflow, wave, and orientation

Surface-mount parts are reflow soldered: paste is printed through a stencil, parts are placed, and the whole board passes through an oven. Through-hole parts, and some surface-mount parts on the bottom side, are wave soldered: the board passes over a standing wave of molten solder. The two processes want different things from the layout.

For wave soldering, orientation relative to the direction of travel decides whether a part gets soldered. Small discrete surface-mount parts should travel through the wave perpendicular to it "so that both pins are soldered simultaneously"; multi-pin surface-mount ICs should be aligned with the wave direction so the leading pins do not shadow the trailing ones; large multi-pin through-hole connectors should also be perpendicular to the wave; and small parts should not sit behind large ones in the direction of travel, or the large part shadows them and leaves a poor joint.[10] Oversized pads bridge in the wave, so follow the IPC land patterns.[10] Bottom-side surface-mount parts close to through-hole pins complicate the wave pallet and can block the wave from the pins.[10]

For reflow, the main orientation issue is thermal balance across a part's pads. If one pad of a small chip component connects to a large copper pour and the other to a thin trace, the pour end heats more slowly; the trace end's paste melts first, surface tension pulls that end up, and the part stands on end. This is tombstoning, and it is the reason for thermal relief.

Thermal relief

A thermal relief connects a pad to a copper plane through a few narrow spokes instead of a solid connection, so that the plane does not pull heat out of the joint while it is being soldered. The Altium guide's summary: thermal reliefs confine heat during soldering to avoid cold joints on through-hole connections and tombstoning; a four-spoke connection is the norm; they are needed on plane-connected surface-mount pads and through-hole pins, not on vias; and in reflow they are needed less often than in hand soldering.[12] JLCPCB's guidance gives four spokes as the practical maximum, spoke widths of 0.10 to 0.20 mm for fine-pitch passives, 0.20 to 0.30 mm for general surface-mount parts, and 0.30 to 0.50 mm for through-hole pins, with anti-pad gaps of about 0.20 to 0.60 mm depending on the class, and a direct connection for any pad carrying more than 3 A continuously or for the exposed pad under a power package, which should connect through a via array instead.[11] The trade-off is exactly as stated: relief protects the joint, a solid connection carries current and heat. Decide per pad.

Panelization, fiducials, and tooling holes

Boards are assembled in panels with breakaway rails that the conveyor and fixtures grip. PCBWay's instructions: rails at least 3 mm wide, with 5, 6, 7, 8, or 10 mm as standard offerings; at least three fiducial marks per panel arranged in an L shape, on both sides if both sides are populated; fiducials of 1.0 to 3.0 mm diameter in bare copper with a clear area of radius at least 2R (3R preferred) that solder mask must not cover, placed at least 5 mm from the board edge; and tooling holes of about 2 mm, non-plated, for positioning and securing the panel.[9] Eurocircuits' advice on fiducials agrees and adds detail: a copper diameter between 1 and 2 mm with 1.5 mm ideal and 0.6 mm the minimum, a mask clearance of 1:2 to 1:3 relative to the copper, three global fiducials near the corners as far apart as possible so the machine can correct for offset, rotation, and stretch, and two local fiducials placed diagonally across each fine-pitch component when the machine needs them.[8]

Decide the panel with the assembler, not after the board is done. Rails need clearance under overhanging components, V-cut edges need 0.4 mm of copper clearance rather than 0.2 mm,[6] tab-routed edges need tabs where no component sits, and fiducials must be visible with the panel loaded either way up.

What the board house checks, and how to read it

Every fabricator runs the design through a DFM check before quoting or building, and the output is a list of violations against their capability: trace and space, annular ring, drill-to-copper, mask dam, silkscreen overlap, copper to edge, aspect ratio, and missing or ambiguous data in the fabrication files.[5] Read the report the way Module 14 describes reading any supplier DFM review. Violations of the fabricator's standard tier are usually fixable in layout at no cost. Violations that push the board into a higher capability tier are a price decision. And the items marked "will be adjusted" (mask openings ganged, silkscreen clipped, teardrops added) are the fabricator changing your design; know what they are before you approve them.

On the assembly side, the equivalent check covers stencil aperture design, component orientation and spacing for the placement machine, reflow profile compatibility of every part (a part that cannot survive the reflow temperature is a hand-soldering operation), fiducial visibility, and inspection access. Automated optical inspection (AOI) sees only surfaces; joints hidden under components, such as those on bottom-terminated packages, need X-ray inspection.[13] Module 11 covers test access.

Component selection and lifecycle risk

A PCBA is only buildable while its components are. Electronic component manufacturers signal the end of a part's life through product change notifications and product discontinuance notices; the industry standard for these is J-STD-048 (formerly JESD48), published jointly by JEDEC, IPC, and ECIA, which sets requirements for timely notification so customers can arrange last-time buys, qualify alternates, or redesign.[16] A part marked "not recommended for new designs" (NRND) is still in production but on notice; designing it in is a redesign scheduled for later. Practical rules: check the lifecycle status of every active component before the layout is frozen; prefer parts with a second source in the same footprint; for critical parts, keep an approved alternate on the BOM; and treat a single-source connector or sensor as a program risk with an owner, not a footnote.

Materials inside components carry their own risk, which is where Galaxy IV comes back. Whiskers grow from pure tin plating on leads, terminals, and relay contacts, and the documented failures span satellites, pacemakers, missiles, and nuclear plant relays across decades.[14] NASA maintains a whisker failure database for that reason.[15] For high-reliability or long-life products, the plating finish on component terminations is a specification, not a detail: ask for it, and treat pure tin with the suspicion the record justifies.

Real-world example: Galaxy IV and the tin whisker

The NASA briefing that documents the Galaxy IV loss is a history of electrical failures caused by metal whiskers, prepared for the Space Shuttle programme in 2006.[14] Its table of documented failures runs from cadmium whiskers in 1959 telecom equipment, through the 1986 recall of heart pacemakers for tin whiskers on crystal cans, to a series of satellite control-processor failures: Galaxy IV (complete loss, 1998), Galaxy VII (1998 and 2000, complete loss), Solidaridad 1 (1999 and 2000, complete loss), Galaxy IIIR (2001 and 2006), DirecTV 3 (2002), and Optus B1 (2005), all attributed to whiskers on relays, plus repeated relay failures at nuclear power stations from 1987 to 2005.[14]

The mechanism Hughes described in 1998 is the whole DFM lesson: a filament of tin grows from a pure-tin-plated surface until it bridges to something at a different potential.[14] The circuit was correct. The component functioned. The failure was a material inside a purchased part, chosen by someone else, that the system design did not question. The engineering responses that followed, alternative platings, conformal coatings that stay intact, and spacing that a whisker cannot bridge, are component and layout decisions, which is why component selection sits in a DFM module and not only in a reliability one.

The wider point holds for every purchased part on a BOM. The datasheet describes what the part does. It rarely describes what it is made of, how long it will be made, or what happens to it in your process. Those are questions for the DFM review, and for Module 14's supplier conversations.

Common mistakes

  1. Designing to the fabricator's finest capability everywhere. Consequence: a board that must be built at the expensive tier because a few traces are fine, when standard-tier rules would have served. Fix: standard minima plus margin by default; fine-line rules only where pitch forces them, and tell the fabricator which nets those are.
  2. Undersized traces for power. Consequence: hot traces, voltage drop, and in the worst case a burned board. Fix: size with IPC-2221 or IPC-2152, put power on outer layers or heavier copper, and remember internal layers carry about half the current.
  3. Vias in pads without filling. Consequence: solder wicks into the via and the joint is starved. Fix: keep vias out of pads or specify filled and capped vias.
  4. Unbalanced thermal connections on small passives. Consequence: tombstoning in reflow. Fix: thermal relief on plane-connected pads, matched pad geometry both ends.
  5. Wave-soldered parts oriented along the wave, or small parts behind tall ones. Consequence: shadowed joints and rework. Fix: two-pad parts across the direction of travel, multi-pin parts along it, and nothing small in the wake of anything tall.
  6. Panel decided after the layout. Consequence: components overhanging rails, no room for fiducials, V-cuts through copper. Fix: agree rails, fiducials, tooling holes, and edge clearances with the assembler before routing.
  7. No room for probes or inspection. Consequence: an untestable board and joints nobody can see. Fix: leave the probe clearances now and X-ray access for bottom-terminated parts (Module 11).
  8. Designing in a part that is already on notice. Consequence: a redesign within the product's first year. Fix: check lifecycle status and second sources before the layout is frozen, and give every single-source part an owner.

Worked exercise: a power trace, a via, and a panel

This is an illustrative example with invented but realistic numbers, not a real program.

A two-layer board carries a 3 A supply rail on 1 oz (35 µm, 1.37 mil) copper. The allowable temperature rise is 20 °C. The board is 1.6 mm thick, will be assembled in a panel, and has one fine-pitch controller.

Tasks. (a) Using the IPC-2221 relationship, compute the required trace width if the rail runs on an outer layer, and again if it must run on an inner layer of a four-layer version. (b) Choose a via drill and pad for the rail's layer transitions using the fabricator minima in this module, and check the aspect ratio. (c) Specify the panel: rails, fiducials, and tooling holes. (d) Check the 1 A at 10 °C case against the published 0.25 mm figure to confirm you are using the formula correctly.

Show the worked solution

(a) Trace width. Rearrange I = k·ΔT0.44·A0.725 to A = [I / (k·ΔT0.44)]1/0.725. ΔT0.44 = 200.44 = 3.74.

External (k = 0.048): k·ΔT0.44 = 0.179; I / that = 16.7; A = 16.71.379 ≈ 48.7 mil². Width = 48.7 / 1.37 ≈ 35.5 mil ≈ 0.90 mm.

Internal (k = 0.024): k·ΔT0.44 = 0.0897; I / that = 33.5; A = 33.51.379 ≈ 127 mil². Width = 127 / 1.37 ≈ 92 mil ≈ 2.35 mm. The internal trace needs about 2.6 times the width for the same current and rise, which is why the rail belongs on an outer layer or on 2 oz copper. For a design where the width matters, repeat with IPC-2152.

(b) Via. A single 0.15 mm via would carry the rail poorly; use several 0.3 mm vias in parallel (a common practice, sized here for the fabricator's capability rather than by a current rule). Pad diameter = drill + 2 × ring = 0.3 + 2 × 0.25 = 0.8 mm using the recommended 0.25 mm ring. Aspect ratio = 1.6 / 0.3 = 5.3 : 1, well inside the 10 : 1 limit. Keep the vias out of the component pads.

(c) Panel. Rails of 5 mm on the two long edges (one standard offering above the 3 mm minimum), with the 3 mm conveyor strip inside the board outline kept free of tall parts. Three global fiducials in an L shape on each populated side, 1.5 mm bare copper with a 1.5 mm radius clear area (2R) free of mask, each at least 5 mm from the board edge and as far apart as the board allows. Two local fiducials placed diagonally across the fine-pitch controller. Two 2 mm non-plated tooling holes in the rails, diagonally opposite. Copper kept 0.4 mm from V-cut lines if the panel is scored, 0.2 mm if it is tab-routed.

(d) Check. 1 A, 10 °C, external: 100.44 = 2.75; 0.048 × 2.75 = 0.132; 1 / 0.132 = 7.57; A = 7.571.379 ≈ 16.3 mil²; width ≈ 11.9 mil ≈ 0.30 mm. The published table value is about 0.25 mm (10 mil); the difference is rounding and the copper thickness assumed (some tables use 1.4 mil for 1 oz). The formula is being applied correctly; either value is a reasonable design minimum for that current, and the fabricator's 0.10 mm process minimum is not the limiting rule here.

Quiz

Eight questions. Score 70 % or more to mark this module complete on this device.

1. Which IPC document is the generic printed board design standard that the trace-current relationship comes from?

IPC-2221B (2012) is the generic design standard; 7351B is land patterns, A-610H is assembly acceptability, J-STD-048 is discontinuance notification.

2. For the same current and temperature rise, an internal trace compared with an external one needs

Halving k roughly doubles the required area for the same current, and the width scales with it.

3. A via has a 0.30 mm drill and the fabricator's recommended 0.25 mm annular ring. What pad diameter in mm is needed?

0.30 + 2 × 0.25 = 0.80 mm.

4. With a maximum aspect ratio of 10 : 1, what is the smallest through-hole drill in mm for a 1.6 mm board?

1.6 / 10 = 0.16 mm.

5. In wave soldering, a small two-pad chip component should be oriented

Perpendicular to the wave so neither pad is shadowed; multi-pin ICs, by contrast, go along the direction of travel.

6. Tombstoning of a small passive in reflow is caused by

Typically one pad is tied to a large copper pour; thermal relief balances the heating.

7. How many global fiducials does a panel need, and where?

Three in an L shape, bare copper, 1.0 to 3.0 mm, with a clear area and at least 5 mm from the edge.

8. The 1998 loss of the Galaxy IV satellite was attributed by its manufacturer to

Hughes' August 1998 statement identified tin-plated relay switches as the most likely cause; the whisker bridged a relay terminal to its case.

Answer key
  1. b. IPC-2221B.
  2. c. Considerably wider; k is halved.
  3. 0.80 mm.
  4. 0.16 mm.
  5. a. Pads across the direction of travel.
  6. d. Unbalanced pad heating.
  7. b. At least three, far apart.
  8. c. Tin whisker on a relay.

Key takeaways

  • Know which IPC document governs what: 2221 for design, 7351 for land patterns, 2231 for DFX practice, A-610 for acceptance. State the acceptance class.
  • Design to the fabricator's standard tier with margin; use fine-line rules only where pitch forces them, and size power traces by current, on outer layers where possible.
  • Annular ring, drill size, and aspect ratio are what make a via reliable. Keep vias out of pads unless they are filled and capped.
  • Solder mask dams and silkscreen clearances have real minima; below them the fabricator changes your design.
  • Orient parts for the soldering process: across the wave for two-pad parts, along it for ICs, thermal relief where a plane would steal heat, direct connection where current matters.
  • Plan the panel with the assembler: rails, three fiducials in bare copper, tooling holes, and edge clearances before routing.
  • A component is a design decision with a lifecycle and a material inside it. Galaxy IV was lost to a plating choice nobody on the schematic side made.
