Module 12

Design for service, reliability, and compliance

A product's life begins when it ships. Design for service (DFS) decides what happens when it fails, design for reliability decides how often, and compliance decides whether it may be sold at all. All three are set by decisions made in the first weeks of a program, and all three are expensive to revisit after tooling. This module gives you the rules that reach back into early design.

Learning objectives

  • Define field-replaceable units and design access, fastening, and diagnostics so that the parts most likely to fail can be replaced first and without damage.
  • Apply published derating factors to electrical components and compute junction temperature margins.
  • Select environmental test methods from the IEC 60068 series that match the product's service conditions.
  • Explain how CE marking, product safety standards, and ingress protection ratings constrain enclosure, insulation, and sealing design.
  • Check a bill of materials against RoHS substance limits and REACH communication duties, using the homogeneous-material and article definitions correctly.
  • Identify when potable-water contact standards apply and what they require of wetted materials.

Why this matters

On 23 January 2017 Samsung Electronics held a press conference to announce the cause of the Galaxy Note7 incidents that had led to two recalls of the phone the previous autumn. The company's investigation, and independent investigations by UL, Exponent, and TÜV Rheinland, concluded that the batteries were the cause.[1] Two battery suppliers were involved and their defects were different: in the first, the negative electrode was deflected at a corner of the cell; in the second, a welding defect during manufacture led to an internal short.[2] Samsung's response was an eight-point battery safety check applied to every battery, covering durability, visual, X-ray, charge and discharge, leak, disassembly, accelerated usage, and voltage tests, and a Battery Advisory Group of outside experts.[1]

The public record does not include the internal engineering decisions, and this course does not speculate about them. What the record shows is the shape of a reliability and compliance failure: a purchased component, qualified and tested, failed in the field at a rate that ended the product; two different suppliers failed in two different ways; and the remedy was a test regime that the original process had not included. The lessons are the ones in this module. Reliability is a property of margins, not of a passed test; environmental and abuse testing must reflect the service the product will see; and supplier processes are part of your product's reliability whether you inspect them or not.

You will meet these disciplines as constraints that appear late and cost a lot: a regulatory requirement that changes an enclosure after tooling, a derating rule that changes a component after the board is laid out, a service procedure that needs a cover to come off that was designed to snap shut forever. The purpose of this module is to move them to the front.

Design for service

Design for service starts with an honest list of what will fail. Wear parts (seals, filters, belts, brushes, batteries), stressed parts (fans, pumps, relays, connectors that are cycled), and parts exposed to the environment (gaskets, displays, keypads) fail first. Each of these is a candidate field-replaceable unit (FRU): a sub-assembly that a technician, or the customer, can swap without special tools and without disturbing the rest of the product.

Rules for FRUs

  • Access order. The part most likely to fail comes out first. Reverse the last steps of assembly (Module 10), not the first; a fuse behind the motherboard is a design error.
  • Fasteners that survive service. Module 10 removes fasteners for assembly; DFS puts a few back where a joint must open repeatedly. The Boothroyd Dewhurst criteria allow it explicitly: a part may stay separate if it "must stay separate to allow assembly sequence, serviceability, or adjustment".[14] Choose one fastener type and size for all service access, captive where a dropped screw would be lost in the product, and avoid snap fits that break when opened.
  • No damage on disassembly. Adhesives, heat stakes, and one-way snaps are assembly-friendly and service-hostile. If a joint must be opened in the field, it must reclose to specification.
  • Connectors, not solder. A FRU connects electrically through a connector with a positive latch and a keyed orientation (poka-yoke again).
  • Diagnostics that point at the FRU. A fault indication that says which unit to replace turns a service call into a swap. Design the self-test to the FRU boundaries.
  • Spare parts as products. A FRU is a stocked, packaged, documented part with its own number, and its availability period is a design commitment.

The regulatory push

Serviceability is becoming a legal requirement in some markets. Directive (EU) 2024/1799 on common rules promoting the repair of goods, adopted 13 June 2024, requires manufacturers to repair goods that are subject to repairability requirements under specified EU legislation when a consumer requests it (unless repair is impossible), introduces a voluntary European Repair Information Form valid for at least 30 days, and extends the seller's liability period once by 12 months when repair is chosen as the remedy; Member States must apply it from 31 July 2026.[13] The design consequence is that for covered product categories, disassembly, spare-part availability, and repair documentation are compliance deliverables, not goodwill.

Design for reliability basics

Reliability engineering is a discipline of its own; this section gives the three habits that belong in every mechanical or electromechanical design review.

Derating

Derating means operating a component well below its rated stress. NASA's preferred reliability practice puts it plainly: derate applied stress levels with respect to the maximum ratings of the part, because "derating lowers the probability of failures occurring during assembly, test, and flight", and data on temperature, voltage, and current stress "indicates decreasing failure rates for lower levels of stress".[3] Its typical guideline values are the ones in Table 1. The Navy's SD-18 parts guide serves the same purpose for defence equipment.[4] These are aerospace and military factors; a consumer product may use less conservative ones, but it should use some, written down, and checked at design review.

Table 1. Typical part derating guidelines (NASA Goddard preferred practice PD-ED-1201).[3]
Part typeRecommended derating level
CapacitorsMaximum 60 % of rated voltage
ResistorsMaximum 60 % of rated power
Semiconductor devicesMaximum 50 % of rated power; maximum 75 % of rated voltage; maximum junction temperature 110 °C
MicrocircuitsMaximum supply voltage 80 % of rated; maximum 75 % of rated power; maximum junction temperature 100 °C
Inductive devicesMaximum 50 % of rated voltage; maximum 60 % of rated temperature
Relays and connectorsMaximum 50 % of rated current

Thermal design

Temperature is the stress that shortens electronic life most reliably, and junction temperature is where it acts. The derating table's limits (110 °C for semiconductors, 100 °C for microcircuits) are reached through the thermal path: Tj = Tambient + P × Rθ, where Rθ is the total thermal resistance from junction to ambient through the package, the board, and the enclosure. Every enclosure decision, from vent area to wall material to where the power components sit relative to the airflow, changes Rθ, which is why thermal design belongs to the mechanical engineer as much as the electrical one. Compute the junction temperatures at the maximum ambient the product will see, not the ambient of the lab.

Environmental testing

The product must survive its environment before it can be reliable in it, and the IEC 60068 series is the common vocabulary for the tests. Its Part 2 methods include sinusoidal vibration (60068-2-6), random vibration (60068-2-64), change of temperature or thermal cycling (60068-2-14), and cyclic damp heat with condensation (60068-2-30, in a 12 h plus 12 h cycle).[5] The DVT plan (Module 1) should name the method, the severity, and the duration for each environment the product will meet: transport vibration, thermal cycling between the coldest storage and the hottest operation, and damp heat for anything that will see condensation. The mechanical design consequences are direct: potting or staking for heavy components under vibration, expansion allowances and compliant joints for thermal cycling, and drainage, coatings, and material choices for damp heat (Module 13).

Failure modes first

The 787 battery case in Module 11 turned on a failure mode that was not in the safety assessment. A design failure mode and effects analysis (DFMEA), which is one of the eighteen elements of the automotive PPAP package,[15] is the structured way to list what can fail, how badly, and what the design does about it, and it feeds both the reliability tests and the service plan. Run it at architecture, before there is a part to protect.

Compliance that reaches into design

Regulatory requirements are often treated as a certification step at the end. Most of them are design constraints at the beginning: they decide materials, insulation distances, enclosure openings, seals, and labels. The ones below are the set a mechanical or electromechanical product in the European and North American markets is most likely to meet.

CE marking and the Blue Guide

Products sold in the European Economic Area under the harmonised product legislation carry the CE mark as the manufacturer's declaration that the product meets the applicable requirements. The European Commission's Blue Guide on the implementation of EU product rules (2022 edition, published 29 June 2022) is the reference for how the system works: conformity assessment, the role of harmonised standards, the obligations of manufacturers, importers, and distributors, CE marking, and market surveillance; it is a non-binding guide, not law, and the 2022 edition added treatment of distance sales and software updates.[8] For a designer, the practical questions are which directives or regulations apply to the product (low voltage, electromagnetic compatibility, machinery, radio equipment, RoHS, and others), which harmonised standards give the presumption of conformity, and which of those standards' requirements are geometry: creepage and clearance distances, enclosure openings, guard dimensions, and temperature limits.

Product safety standards

For household and similar appliances, the base safety standard is IEC 60335-1, whose sixth edition (2020) covers appliances with a rated voltage not more than 250 V for single-phase and 480 V for others, including battery-operated appliances; the 2020 edition added requirements for battery charging of metal-ion cells, new test probe applications, optical radiation hazards, and a normative annex on cybersecurity.[7] Part 2 standards add product-specific requirements. In North America the equivalent role is played by the UL and CSA standards, many of which are harmonised with their IEC counterparts, and by the requirement for listing by a nationally recognised testing laboratory. The design content of these standards is concrete: how far a live part must be from a touchable surface, how small an enclosure opening must be to exclude a test probe, how hot a handle may get, what a cord anchorage must withstand. Read the applicable standard at architecture, because each of those numbers is a wall, a gap, or a rib in the CAD.

Ingress protection: IEC 60529

The IP code of IEC 60529 (current consolidated edition 2.2, 2013, applying to enclosures for equipment rated up to 72.5 kV) classifies the degree of protection an enclosure provides against access to hazardous parts, ingress of solid foreign objects, and ingress of water.[6] The IEC summarises the code as two numerals: the first "refers to the protection against solid objects and is rated on a scale from 0 (no protection) to 6 (no ingress of dust)", and the second "rates the enclosure's protection against liquids and uses a scale from 0 (no protection) to 9 (high-pressure hot water from different angles)".[16] Between those ends the second-numeral tests run from dripping water through spray and jets to temporary and continuous immersion. The design consequences are the whole enclosure: gasket type and compression, joint geometry, fastener spacing to hold gasket pressure, drain and breather features to manage the pressure changes that pull water past a seal, connector and switch ratings that match the enclosure, and a test method that can be run in production (Module 11). Specify the IP rating the application needs and no more; every step up costs gasket area, fastener count, and test time.

Restricted substances: RoHS

Directive 2011/65/EU (RoHS 2, 8 June 2011) restricts the use of certain substances in electrical and electronic equipment. Its Annex II sets maximum concentration values by weight in homogeneous materials: 0.1 percent for lead, mercury, hexavalent chromium, polybrominated biphenyls (PBB), and polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDE), and 0.01 percent for cadmium.[9] Four phthalate plasticisers (DEHP, BBP, DBP, DIBP) were added at 0.1 percent by Delegated Directive (EU) 2015/863.[9] The definition that decides everything is "homogeneous material": "one material of uniform composition throughout or a material, consisting of a combination of materials, that cannot be disjointed or separated into different materials by mechanical actions such as unscrewing, cutting, crushing, grinding and abrasive processes".[9] The limit applies to each plating layer, each solder joint, each plastic, not to the part or the product as a whole, so a 1 g plating layer with 0.2 percent lead fails on a 500 g part that is otherwise lead-free.

Design consequences: lead-free solder (which changed reflow temperatures and, through tin-rich finishes, revived the tin-whisker concern of Module 9), plating specifications that name the alloy, plasticiser-free flexible plastics, and a supplier declaration for every homogeneous material. Exemptions exist for specific applications and are time-limited; check the current annexes rather than assuming.

Substances in articles: REACH

Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006 (REACH) governs chemicals in the EU, and two of its articles reach mechanical designers. Article 33 requires any supplier of an article containing a substance of very high concern (SVHC) from the Candidate List at more than 0.1 percent by weight to give the recipient enough information to allow safe use, at least the name of the substance, and to give the same to a consumer on request within 45 days; Article 7(2) requires notification to the European Chemicals Agency when such substances in articles exceed one tonne per producer or importer per year.[10] Unlike RoHS, the 0.1 percent applies to the article, and the Candidate List grows twice a year, so a compliant material can become a communication obligation later. The design habit is a full material declaration for every purchased part and a check of the Candidate List at each release.

Potable water contact: NSF/ANSI/CAN 61 and 372

Products that touch drinking water in North America are evaluated to NSF/ANSI/CAN 61, which covers most products in contact with drinking water, including pipes and fittings, valves, pumps, water meters, faucets, joining and sealing materials, coatings and liners, process media, and plastic materials, and tests them for chemical contaminants that leach into the water; the companion standard NSF/ANSI/CAN 372 requires a calculated weighted-average lead content of no more than 0.25 percent for products that convey or dispense drinking water.[11] NSF/ANSI/CAN 61 does not set performance, taste and odour, or microbial growth requirements; those are other standards.[12] The design consequence is that every wetted material, including gaskets, lubricants, adhesives, and coatings, is a compliance decision, and a material change on a wetted part after certification is a recertification.

A compliance-driven design checklist

  1. List the markets and the applicable rules at concept: directives and regulations, harmonised standards, safety standards with their Part 2, water-contact standards, and the repair rules for the product category.
  2. Extract the geometry: creepage and clearance, enclosure openings, guard sizes, temperature limits, IP rating, gasket and fastener requirements.
  3. Extract the materials: RoHS homogeneous-material limits, REACH Candidate List, water-contact approval, flammability ratings where the standard calls for them.
  4. Derate every stressed component against a written policy, and compute junction temperatures at the maximum service ambient.
  5. Write the DVT environmental test plan in IEC 60068 terms from the failure-mode list, including the worst credible case.
  6. Define the FRUs and their access, and design the service fasteners and connectors before the DFA pass removes them.
  7. Collect declarations from every supplier and keep the technical file current through every engineering change (Module 14).

Real-world example: two batteries, two defects

Samsung's 23 January 2017 announcement concluded, with the concurrence of UL, Exponent, and TÜV Rheinland, that the batteries caused the Galaxy Note7 incidents.[1] Press accounts of the presentation describe two distinct root causes at two suppliers: a deflection of the negative electrode in one battery design, and in the replacement battery a defect introduced during welding that led to an internal short.[2] Samsung's response included an eight-point battery safety check and an external advisory group.[1]

Three points for a designer. First, reliability of a purchased component is a property of its manufacturing process, not only its design; two suppliers building to the same specification produced two different defects. Module 14's supplier oversight and Module 11's inspection-capability lesson apply. Second, replacing a failed component with another built the same way and tested the same way is not a fix; the second recall followed the first because the test regime had not changed. Third, margins at product level (mechanical clearance around a cell, thermal design, abuse tests that represent service) are the designer's contribution to a component's reliability, and they are set in the architecture, where this module started.

The record does not say what the internal design margins were, and the course does not guess. It does show that the final remedy was a test and inspection programme far more extensive than the original, which is the cost of adding reliability engineering after the fact.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating compliance as certification. Consequence: an enclosure redesign after tooling to gain a creepage distance or an IP test. Fix: extract the geometry and material requirements from the applicable standards at architecture.
  2. Components at their rating. Consequence: early failures at the top of the ambient range. Fix: a written derating policy applied at design review, and junction temperatures computed at maximum ambient.
  3. Environmental tests chosen for convenience. Consequence: the product passes the lab and fails the truck. Fix: derive IEC 60068 methods and severities from the service environment and the failure-mode list.
  4. The DFA pass removed the service fasteners. Consequence: a snap-fit housing broken open in the field. Fix: define FRUs and their access before the fastener reduction, and keep one standard, captive fastener type for service joints.
  5. RoHS checked per part instead of per homogeneous material. Consequence: a compliant-looking part with a non-compliant plating layer. Fix: declarations at the material level, and a supplier who understands the definition.
  6. REACH checked once. Consequence: a material added to the Candidate List after launch creates an undisclosed communication duty. Fix: full material declarations and a Candidate List check at every release.
  7. Wetted material changed without recertification. Consequence: a product that no longer meets its drinking-water listing. Fix: treat every wetted material as a certified material and route changes through the compliance owner.
  8. Specifying a higher IP rating than the application needs. Consequence: gasket cost, fastener count, breather features, and a longer production leak test on every unit. Fix: rate to the environment and design the test alongside (Module 11).

Worked exercise: derating a power board and rating its enclosure

This is an illustrative example with invented but realistic numbers, not a real program.

A small controller runs from a 12 V rail inside an enclosure whose internal air reaches 55 °C at the maximum specified ambient. Components under review: an aluminium electrolytic capacitor rated 16 V on the 12 V rail; a ceramic capacitor rated 25 V on the same rail; a 0.25 W resistor dissipating 0.18 W; a MOSFET dissipating 0.8 W with a total junction-to-ambient thermal resistance of 60 K/W as mounted; a relay with 5 A rated contacts switching a 3 A load. The enclosure must be dust-tight and survive temporary immersion during washdown. One cable jacket is flexible PVC containing 2 percent DEHP by weight.

Tasks. (a) Check each component against the NASA derating table and propose changes. (b) Compute the MOSFET junction temperature and its margin. (c) State the IP code and list the enclosure design consequences. (d) Assess the cable jacket against RoHS.

Show the worked solution

(a) Derating. Electrolytic at 16 V on 12 V: 75 percent of rating, above the 60 percent limit; change to 25 V (48 percent). Ceramic at 25 V: 48 percent, acceptable. Resistor 0.18 / 0.25 = 72 percent, above 60 percent; change to 0.5 W (36 percent) or reduce dissipation. Relay 3 / 5 = 60 percent, above the 50 percent limit for relays and connectors; change to a relay rated at least 6 A, or reduce the load to 2.5 A. MOSFET: see (b) for temperature; also check that its power and voltage are at or below 50 percent and 75 percent of rating respectively.

(b) Junction temperature. Tj = 55 + 0.8 × 60 = 55 + 48 = 103 °C. The limit for semiconductors is 110 °C, so it passes by 7 K, which is thin: a 10 K rise in enclosure temperature (a blocked vent, a hotter summer) consumes the margin. Options: lower Rθ with copper area or a heat spreader to the enclosure wall (a 20 K/W improvement brings Tj to 87 °C), reduce dissipation with a lower-resistance device, or reduce the internal air temperature with the enclosure design. The mechanical engineer owns two of those three options.

(c) IP rating. Dust-tight is first numeral 6; temporary immersion is second numeral 7, so IP67. Consequences: a continuous gasket in a groove around the lid with fastener spacing close enough to hold compression along the whole length; sealed cable glands or moulded connectors rated to the same level; a breather membrane or a design that tolerates the pressure differential of immersion after heating, so that cooling does not draw water past the seal; a production leak test (Module 11) with a test port and clamping datum; and switches, displays, and any user interface rated for the same immersion. If the washdown is really a hose rather than immersion, a second numeral for water jets may suffice and would cost less; confirm the service environment before committing.

(d) RoHS. The cable jacket is a homogeneous material. DEHP is restricted at 0.1 percent by weight since Delegated Directive (EU) 2015/863; 2 percent is twenty times the limit, so the cable is non-compliant and must be replaced with a phthalate-free jacket. The cable also needs a supplier declaration at the material level, and if it is an article containing a Candidate List substance above 0.1 percent it carries a REACH Article 33 communication duty as well.

Key takeaways

  • List what will fail, make those parts field-replaceable, and design their access, fasteners, and connectors before the DFA pass removes them. In the EU, repairability is becoming law for covered products.
  • Derate against a written policy (NASA's factors are a conservative reference), and compute junction temperatures at the hottest ambient the product will see. The enclosure is part of the thermal path.
  • Write the environmental test plan in IEC 60068 terms from the failure-mode list, including the worst credible case.
  • Compliance is geometry and materials decided at architecture: creepage and openings from the safety standard, seals and fasteners from the IP rating, plating and plasticisers from RoHS, wetted materials from the water-contact standard.
  • RoHS limits apply per homogeneous material; REACH duties apply per article and change as the Candidate List grows. Collect material declarations, not just compliance letters.
  • A purchased component's reliability is a property of its supplier's process. Two suppliers to one specification produced two different defects in the Note7.
  • Specify the protection the application needs and no more. Every extra requirement is paid for on every unit.
