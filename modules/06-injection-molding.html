Module 6

DFM for injection molding

Injection moulding is the process where design decisions are hardest to undo. The mould is cut in steel before you have seen a part, and every wall, rib, and radius you draw becomes a cost the moment the tool is ordered. This module gives you the rules, the reasons behind them, and the arithmetic to argue for them.

Learning objectives

  • Specify a nominal wall thickness for a given resin and keep adjacent walls within the ratio that avoids sink, warp, and voids.
  • Calculate rib, boss, fillet, and spacing dimensions from the nominal wall, and estimate how a wall-thickness change moves cooling time and material cost.
  • Apply draft angles by feature depth and surface texture, and explain why textured surfaces need more.
  • Identify undercuts and decide between a side action, a redesign, or a change of parting line.
  • Explain where sink marks, weld lines, and warp come from and which design choices cause them.
  • Choose a tolerance, a surface finish, and a tooling class with an understanding of what each one costs.

Why this matters

A machined part that comes back wrong can be fixed by changing a program. A moulded part that comes back wrong means a tool change, and the direction of the change decides the cost. Removing steel from a mould (which adds plastic to the part) is a machining operation. Adding steel to a mould (which removes plastic from the part) means welding, inserts, or a new cavity block. Moulders call the cheap direction "steel-safe" and design their first cut so that the likely corrections go that way. If your part needs a wall to get thinner after first shots, you are asking for the expensive direction.

The precision that good moulding can reach is real. The LEGO Group's own history notes that its 1963 switch from cellulose acetate to ABS permitted moulding "to an accuracy of 1/200 mm", which is 0.005 mm.[10] That precision is bought with tooling quality, process control, and a part design that gives the process a chance: uniform walls, generous draft, and features that cool at the same rate.

The cheaper lesson comes from a study of eighteen automatic drip coffee makers by Ulrich and Pearson at MIT. One maker's water tank was moulded in inexpensive polypropylene, which shows sink marks where walls meet. Rather than switch to polycarbonate, a resin the authors estimated at two to three times the price, the designers hid the sink with a ribbed pattern on the tank surface.[11],[12] That is DFM working as intended: the physics of the process was accepted and the design absorbed it, at almost no cost.

How the process shapes the rules

Molten polymer is injected under high pressure into a steel cavity, packed to compensate for shrinkage as it cools, held until it is stiff enough to survive ejection, then pushed out by ejector pins when the mould opens. Almost every design rule in this module comes from one of four facts about that cycle.

  1. The part must come out. The mould opens in one direction. Any feature that would trap the part in either half is an undercut and needs a moving mould component or a redesign. Walls parallel to the opening direction drag on the steel unless they are tapered (draft).
  2. Cooling dominates the cycle. The hot plastic loses heat through the mould walls, so the thickest section sets the cooling time. For a plate cooling by conduction, the cooling time to a given centre temperature is proportional to the square of the thickness.[6] A 3 mm wall does not cool 50 % slower than a 2 mm wall; it cools 2.25 times slower.
  3. Plastic shrinks as it cools, and thick sections shrink last. Where a thick section meets a thin one, the thin section has already frozen when the thick section is still pulling inward. The result is a sink mark on the surface or a void inside.
  4. Everything about the tool is expensive and slow. Aluminium prototype moulds and production steel moulds differ by an order of magnitude in life, and a hardened multi-cavity production tool can take months. Features that need side actions, lifters, or hand-loaded inserts add cost and cycle time for the life of the tool.

Wall thickness and uniformity

Wall thickness is the first number you choose, and most other dimensions in the part scale from it. Thicker walls are stiffer and fill more easily, but they add material, cycle time, and sink. Thinner walls save all three but fill harder, especially far from the gate and in viscous resins. Suppliers publish typical ranges by resin; Protolabs' are shown in Table 1.[1],[2]

Within a part, the rule that matters more than the absolute value is uniformity. Protolabs' guidance is that walls should be no less than 40 to 60 percent of the adjacent wall, and that secondary features such as ribs and bosses should sit at roughly 40 to 60 percent of the surrounding wall.[2] Non-uniform walls drive sink, warp, internal stress, and dimensional error, because the thick regions are still shrinking after the thin ones have frozen.[2] When a region must be thick for function, core it out from the back so that the wall is uniform and the stiffness comes from ribs rather than mass. Covestro's design guide makes the economic point directly: increasing wall thickness adds part weight, cycle time, and material cost, and geometric features such as ribs, curves, and corrugations can add stiffness at very little of that cost.[5]

Cooling time arithmetic. The one-dimensional conduction estimate used by resin suppliers gives cooling time tc = (ℓ² / π²α) · ln[(θ0 − θs) / (θ − θs)], where ℓ is the wall thickness, α the thermal diffusivity of the polymer, θ0 the melt temperature, θs the mould temperature, and θ the ejection temperature.[6] Everything except ℓ² is fixed by the resin and process settings, so the ratio of cooling times between two designs is simply the ratio of thickness squared. Going from 3.0 mm to 2.5 mm cuts cooling time to (2.5/3.0)² = 0.69 of the original.

Radii, ribs, bosses, and gussets

Sharp internal corners concentrate stress in the part, concentrate stress in the tool steel, and create a locally thick section where the two walls overlap. The Hubs design guide recommends an internal radius of 0.5 times the wall thickness and an external radius of 1.5 times the wall, which keeps the wall thickness constant around the corner.[4]

Ribs add stiffness without adding wall. The rule set from the same guide: rib thickness equal to 0.5 times the main wall, rib height less than 3 times the rib thickness, a fillet at the rib base greater than a quarter of the rib thickness, and at least 4 times the rib thickness between adjacent ribs and walls.[4] The thickness ratio is the sink control: a rib at half the wall creates an intersection only modestly thicker than the wall, so the surface opposite the rib sinks little. Suppliers vary on the height limit; some allow taller ribs with more draft and a thicker base. Ask yours, and expect them to want draft on both faces of any rib taller than a few millimetres.

Bosses are hollow cylinders that receive screws or pins. Hubs recommends an outer diameter of 2 times the nominal screw diameter and an inner diameter equal to the screw's core diameter.[4] A boss standing alone in the middle of a wall is a thick section and a sink mark waiting to happen, so connect it to the nearest wall with a gusset rib and, if it is tall, core the wall behind it.

Draft

Draft is the taper on any face parallel to the mould opening direction. Without it the part drags on the steel as the mould opens or as the ejectors push, which scratches the surface and can bend, break, or warp the part, and in bad cases damages the mould.[3] Texture makes it worse: a textured mould surface is a field of micro-undercuts, and it needs steeper walls to release cleanly.[3]

Published minimums differ, which tells you that draft is a negotiation with your moulder rather than a constant. Protolabs advises at least 0.5° on all vertical faces, says 1° to 2° works in most situations, and requires 3° for a shut-off (steel sliding on steel), 3° for light texture, and 5° or more for medium texture.[1],[3] Its rule of thumb is 1° of draft per 25 mm (1 inch) of depth.[3] Hubs recommends a 2° minimum on all vertical walls, increasing by 1° for every 25 mm of height, plus 1° to 2° extra for textured surfaces.[4] Covestro ties the minimum to the resin: at least 1° for its polycarbonate and PC/ABS grades, 3° or more for high-heat polycarbonate, and at least 5° plus a 0.5 to 0.6 µm surface finish for thermoplastic polyurethane.[5] Soft, grippy, or high-temperature resins need more.

The geometry is simple: a wall of height H with draft angle θ moves H·tan θ at its free end. A 40 mm deep housing wall at 2° moves 40 × tan 2° = 1.4 mm. That is why draft has to be in the CAD model from the first concept, not added at the end: it changes the outside dimensions, the inside clearances, and where mating parts sit.

Parting line, undercuts, and side actions

Look at the part along the opening direction. Anything that would prevent either mould half from pulling straight away, such as a side hole, a snap hook facing sideways, an external groove, or a lip that faces the wrong way, is an undercut. Every undercut needs one of three answers.

  • Move the parting line so the feature is formed by the two halves meeting, or open it into a pass-through so a core from one half forms it. This costs nothing in the tool.
  • Redesign the feature. Replace a side hole with a slot open to the top edge. Turn a sideways snap into one whose hook faces the opening direction. Use a shut-off so a core from the cavity side forms the underside of a hook (which is why shut-offs need 3° of draft, as above).
  • Add a side action (a slide, cam, or lifter that moves before ejection). Protolabs states maximum side-core dimensions of roughly 214 mm wide, 60 mm tall, and 74 mm of pull for its process, which shows the scale of what is routine and what is not.[1] Side actions add tool cost, maintenance, and cycle time for the life of the tool, and they leave witness lines on the part.

Two related marks that surprise first-time designers: ejector pins leave shallow circular witnesses on the core-side surface, and the parting line leaves a fine line all the way round the part. Both can be positioned, neither can be eliminated. Decide early which surfaces are cosmetic and put the pins and the parting line somewhere else.

Gates, flow, weld lines, and vents

The gate is where plastic enters the cavity. Its location decides how the melt flows, how the part packs, and where the surface blemish at the gate sits. Flow fronts that split around a hole or a core and meet again on the far side leave a weld line (also called a knit line): a visible line and a locally weak zone, because the two fronts have cooled before they merge. Every through-hole in a moulded part has a weld line on its downstream side. You cannot remove them, but you can put them where they are neither seen nor loaded, by choosing the gate location and by giving the moulder freedom to place it.

Air in the cavity has to leave as plastic enters. Covestro's guide puts vents first along the parting line, with typical vent depths of 0.025 to 0.05 mm (0.0010 to 0.0020 in), shallow enough that plastic does not flash through them.[5] Deep, closed-bottom pockets and the ends of tall ribs are where air gets trapped; they are also where burns and short shots show up in first samples. If a rib must be tall and blind, expect the moulder to ask for a vent pin or a slight opening.

Shrinkage, sink, and warp

Polymers shrink as they cool from melt to room temperature, and the tool is cut oversize to compensate. The amount depends on the resin. Semi-crystalline polymers such as polypropylene and nylon shrink more than amorphous polymers such as ABS and polycarbonate because their chains pack into an ordered structure on cooling. One moulder's published ranges: ABS 0.4 to 0.7 %, polypropylene 1 to 2 %, PA11 nylon 1 to 2.5 %, with glass filling reducing shrinkage in nylon.[7] Shrinkage data is on every resin datasheet and the tool is cut to the datasheet, so changing resin after the tool is cut is a tool change.

Sink marks are local depressions where a thick section (a rib root, a boss, a corner without a radius) kept shrinking after the surface froze. Warp is the part-scale version: one region cooled and shrank at a different rate from another, so the part bends. Both are controlled by the same design decisions already covered: uniform walls, ribs at half the wall, coring of thick regions, and consistent corner radii. Glass fibre changes the picture because the fibres align with the flow and shrink less along their length than across it, so filled parts warp in ways unfilled ones do not; Covestro's guide devotes discussion to rib thickness versus warpage in filled and unfilled resins.[5]

Tolerances and capability

A moulded dimension has two sources of variation stacked on top of each other: the steel and the plastic. Protolabs quotes a machining tolerance of ±0.08 mm (±0.003 in) on the tool plus a resin tolerance of at least ±0.002 mm per mm of dimension.[1] On a 50 mm dimension that resin term alone is ±0.10 mm before the steel is considered. Hubs' guide gives a standard tolerance of ±0.25 mm (0.010 in), notes that ±0.125 mm is achievable at extra cost, and that ±0.025 mm is possible only in special cases.[4]

This is why a ±0.05 mm tolerance on a moulded part is a cost decision and not a drafting habit. Meeting it means a tool cut to a tighter steel tolerance, a resin with low and stable shrinkage, a process held to a tighter window, more inspection, and usually a lower Cpk than the drawing implies. Recall from Module 3 that a centred process at Cp = 1.00 produces about 0.27 % rejects and at Cp = 1.33 about 64 parts per million.[14] Ask which dimensions actually need the tolerance, and put general tolerances on the rest.

The general-tolerance standards for moulded parts are DIN 16742:2013 and ISO 20457:2018. They define tolerance groups by nominal size and distinguish mould-fixed dimensions (formed within one half of the tool) from non-mould-fixed dimensions (across the parting line or formed by moving components), which get a larger allowance because tool movement adds variation.[13] The same logic appears in die casting (Module 7). If a dimension matters, keep it in one mould half.

Surface finish and texture

Mould finish is specified by the SPI (Society of the Plastics Industry, now the Plastics Industry Association) grades. Group A finishes are diamond-buffed and glossy (A-1 the highest gloss, A-2 the usual high-gloss choice, A-3 a practical polish), group B are paper-sanded (B-1 600 grit, B-2 400, B-3 320), group C are stone-finished (C-1 600, C-2 400, C-3 320), and group D are blasted textures (D-1 glass bead, D-2 fine oxide, D-3 coarse oxide).[9] Polishing is skilled manual work and its cost scales with the area polished, so Ulrich and Pearson's guideline for low-cost parts stands: concentrate high-quality finishes only on surfaces that are visible, and leave inside surfaces at a machined or stoned finish.[11] Texture hides sink, flow marks, and scratches, but it demands the extra draft described earlier.

Material selection for mouldability

Resin choice sets wall-thickness range, shrinkage, draft requirement, and price at the same time. The main families for mechanical parts:

  • Commodity amorphous (ABS, PS): low shrinkage, easy to mould and finish, moderate strength and temperature.
  • Commodity semi-crystalline (PP, PE): cheap, chemically resistant, higher shrinkage, hard to hold tight tolerances, prone to sink.
  • Engineering amorphous (PC, PC/ABS, PMMA): stiff, clear or tough, low shrinkage, higher price. Ulrich and Pearson estimated polycarbonate at two to three times the price of polypropylene.[11]
  • Engineering semi-crystalline (PA, POM, PBT): good wear and fatigue, higher shrinkage, often glass-filled for stiffness at the cost of warp behaviour and tool wear.

Choose the resin with the moulder in the room. A grade change within a family (a different flow index, a filled version) can rescue a part that will not fill or will not hold size, and it is far cheaper than a tool change.

Tooling classes, tool life, and the cost of change

Mould quality is specified by class. The Plastics Industry Association's Customs and Practices of the Moldmaking Industry (document AR-101, first issued 1996 and revised May 2023) defines the classes and their expected lives to standardise quoting.[8] As summarised by moulders, Class 101 tools are built for one million cycles or more, Class 102 for up to one million, Class 103 for up to 500,000, Class 104 for up to 100,000, and Class 105 for prototype use of up to about 500 cycles.[8] The class determines the steel, the hardening, the cooling design, and the price.

For orientation only, Hubs' published bands: aluminium pilot-run moulds at about US$3,000 to 5,000 lasting 5,000 to 10,000 cycles; tool-steel moulds lasting 100,000 cycles or more; pilot runs of 500 to 10,000 parts at roughly US$1 to 5 per part; full production above 100,000 parts at a few cents to US$1 per part; mould fabrication of 4 to 6 weeks and total lead times of 6 to 10 weeks.[4] These are one supplier's figures for small parts and will not match a multi-cavity hot-runner tool for a large housing. Module 15 builds a proper cost model.

The cost of a mould change depends almost entirely on direction. Making a hole smaller, a wall thicker, or a boss taller means removing steel: a machining setup on an existing tool. Making a hole larger, a wall thinner, or removing a rib means adding steel: welding and re-machining, an insert, or a new cavity block, followed by re-polishing and re-texturing anything the work touched. Experienced designers and moulders therefore cut uncertain features steel-safe: holes slightly small, walls slightly thick, snap engagement slightly deep, so that first-shot corrections go the cheap way. Build that expectation into your first-article plan and into which dimensions you flag as critical.

Real-world example: precision and pragmatism in two moulded parts

LEGO bricks. The LEGO Group's own history records that the 1963 change of material to ABS "permits a greater precision in molding to an accuracy of 1/200 mm", and that most elements are still moulded in ABS.[10] Two DFM points sit inside that sentence. First, the precision came from a material change, not only from tooling: ABS is amorphous, low-shrinkage, and dimensionally stable, which is what makes tight, repeatable clutch fits possible. Second, the product was designed for that process from the start, with uniform thin walls, tubes and studs that are small and evenly spaced, and no thick sections. The bricks are an argument that moulding tolerances are a system property of resin, tool, process, and part geometry together, which is exactly why a tolerance requested in isolation on a drawing is so expensive to promise.

A coffee-maker tank. Ulrich and Pearson took apart eighteen automatic drip coffee makers to model their manufacturing cost. Among the design guidelines they drew from the sample: use inexpensive materials (polypropylene dominated; polycarbonate parts cost two to three times as much), minimise material by controlling wall thickness and overall dimensions, minimise the number of parts by consolidating mouldings, and concentrate high-quality finishes only where surfaces are visible.[11] The Braun tank described in the Wharton account of the same research shows the guidelines in action: the polypropylene showed sink lines at wall intersections, and the designers hid them with a ribbed pattern instead of paying for a more expensive resin.[12] The same authors also found that industrial-design complexity correlated with tooling cost and tooling lead time, though at coffee-maker volumes the tooling difference amounted to only a few cents per unit.[11] Whether that trade is worth it depends on your volume, which is the subject of Modules 2 and 15.

Common mistakes

  1. Designing the part as a solid and thinning it later. Consequence: thick sections, sink, long cycles, and a redesign after the moulder's DFM review. Fix: start from a nominal wall and build every feature as a shell of that wall from the first sketch.
  2. Adding draft at the end. Consequence: mating parts no longer fit because outside and inside dimensions moved by H·tan θ. Fix: model draft with the first wall and design mates to the drafted geometry.
  3. Ribs as thick as the wall. Consequence: a sink mark on the show surface opposite every rib. Fix: rib at 0.5× wall with a small base fillet; hide unavoidable sink with texture or a pattern.
  4. Isolated bosses. Consequence: sink opposite the boss, and a boss that flexes or cracks under screw torque. Fix: gusset the boss to the nearest wall and core behind it.
  5. Sideways snaps and holes that create undercuts. Consequence: side actions in the tool for the life of the product. Fix: reorient the feature to the opening direction, use a pass-through core, or move the parting line.
  6. Tight tolerances by habit. Consequence: a tighter and slower tool, extra inspection, and a Cpk the moulder cannot hold. Fix: general tolerances everywhere except the few functional dimensions, and keep those in one mould half.
  7. Specifying a high polish on hidden surfaces. Consequence: hours of hand polishing that nobody sees. Fix: SPI A or B grades only on visible surfaces; C or D grades elsewhere.
  8. Changing resin after the tool is cut. Consequence: shrinkage changes, so every dimension moves and the tool needs rework. Fix: freeze the resin grade before steel is ordered, and involve the moulder in the choice.

Worked exercise: a housing redesign

This is an illustrative example with invented but realistic numbers, not a real program.

An open-top ABS electronics housing measures 120 × 80 × 40 mm on the outside. The first CAD model has a 3.5 mm wall, four stiffening ribs 3.5 mm thick and 12 mm tall on the base, and a free-standing boss for an M3 thread-forming screw with a 9 mm outside diameter. Annual volume is 100,000. The moulder's first quote assumes a 30 s cycle, of which 60 % is cooling, and you may use an illustrative machine rate of €60 per hour and ABS at €2.50 per kg (density 1.04 g/cm³) for the cost estimate.

Tasks. (a) Propose a new nominal wall inside the ABS range and redesign the ribs and boss to the Hubs ratios. (b) Estimate the change in cooling time and cycle time. (c) Estimate the annual machine-time and material savings. (d) State the minimum draft you would put on the 40 mm walls, plain and textured.

Show the worked solution

(a) Wall and features. ABS is quoted at 1.14 to 3.56 mm.[1] Choose 2.5 mm: comfortably inside the range, stiff enough for a housing this size, and enough for the boss. Redesign to the Hubs ratios:[4]

  • Rib thickness 0.5 × 2.5 = 1.25 mm. Rib height under 3 × 1.25 = 3.75 mm per the Hubs limit. The original 12 mm ribs would need to be either shortened, or (with the moulder's agreement) kept taller with a thicker base and 1° or more of draft per face. Spacing at least 4 × 1.25 = 5 mm between ribs and walls. Base fillet at least 0.25 × 1.25 = 0.31 mm; use 0.4 mm.
  • Boss for M3: outside diameter 2 × 3 = 6 mm, hole about 2.5 mm (the screw core diameter for a thread-forming screw; confirm with the fastener datasheet). Boss wall is then (6 − 2.5)/2 = 1.75 mm, which is 70 % of the 2.5 mm wall and above the 40 to 60 % range Protolabs gives for secondary features,[2] so expect a faint sink opposite the boss. Mitigate by adding a gusset to the nearest side wall and coring the base behind the boss, or by accepting the mark on a non-visible base.
  • Corners: internal radius 1.25 mm, external radius 3.75 mm.

(b) Cooling and cycle time. Cooling time scales with thickness squared.[6] Ratio = (2.5 / 3.5)² = 0.51. Original cooling time = 0.6 × 30 s = 18 s; new cooling time ≈ 0.51 × 18 = 9.2 s. The other 12 s of the cycle (fill, pack, open, eject) is assumed unchanged. New cycle ≈ 12 + 9.2 = 21.2 s, a saving of 8.8 s per shot (29 %). In practice the ribs were also thinned, so the thickest section really is the 2.5 mm wall; if the boss wall or a rib root remained thicker than 2.5 mm, that section would set the cooling time instead.

(c) Annual savings. Machine time: 8.8 s × 100,000 = 880,000 s = 244 h per year. At €60/h that is about €14,700 per year in machine time alone (single cavity assumed).

Material: treat the housing as five faces of area 2 × (120 × 40) + 2 × (80 × 40) + 120 × 80 = 9,600 + 6,400 + 9,600 = 25,600 mm². Wall volume at 3.5 mm ≈ 89,600 mm³ = 89.6 cm³ → 93.2 g. At 2.5 mm ≈ 64,000 mm³ → 66.6 g. Saving about 26.6 g per part before ribs and boss are counted. Over 100,000 parts that is 2,660 kg, or about €6,650 per year at €2.50/kg. Combined saving roughly €21,000 per year for a redesign that also reduces sink and warp risk. (These rates are illustrative; use your moulder's quote.)

(d) Draft. The walls are 40 mm deep. Protolabs' rule of thumb of about 1° per 25 mm gives roughly 1.6°, and its depth table puts a 38 mm feature with a wall over 2.5 mm at 1° minimum and 2° preferred;[3] Hubs' 2° minimum plus 1° per 25 mm suggests about 3.6°.[4] A defensible specification is 2° minimum on plain walls, moving to 3° for a light texture and 5° for a medium texture.[1],[3] At 2°, the top of the wall moves 40 × tan 2° = 1.4 mm relative to the base; at 5° it moves 3.5 mm. Whoever designs the lid must know which value was chosen.

Half the wall limits the thick intersection at the rib root, which is what causes sink on the opposite face.

Class 101 is the highest class, built for one million or more cycles; Class 105 is the prototype class.

8. After first shots, which correction is the cheap, "steel-safe" direction?

Adding plastic to the part means removing steel from the mould, which is a machining operation. The other three add steel, which means welding, inserts, or a new block.

Answer key
  1. b. Cooling time scales with thickness squared.
  2. a. Rib thickness about 0.5 × wall.
  3. 1.0 mm.
  4. c. 3° for light texture.
  5. d. Polypropylene, a semi-crystalline resin.
  6. ±0.12 mm.
  7. a. One million cycles or more.
  8. b. Thickening a wall removes steel from the mould.

Key takeaways

  • Pick a nominal wall inside the resin's range and build every feature as a shell of that wall. Uniformity matters more than the absolute value.
  • Cooling time goes with thickness squared. The thickest section, not the average, sets the cycle time.
  • Ribs at half the wall, bosses at twice the screw diameter tied to a wall, and constant-thickness corners are the sink and warp controls.
  • Draft is part of the design, not a finishing touch. Use at least 0.5° to 2° on plain walls, 3° on shut-offs and light texture, 5° on medium texture, and more for soft or high-temperature resins.
  • Every undercut costs a side action for the life of the tool. Reorient, core through, or move the parting line first.
  • A moulded tolerance is steel variation plus resin variation. Tighten only functional dimensions and keep them in one mould half.
  • Mould changes are cheap in one direction and expensive in the other. Design steel-safe and plan first-article corrections accordingly.
