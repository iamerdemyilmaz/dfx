Module 11

Design for test

A product that cannot be tested cannot be trusted, and a product that can only be tested after it is sealed will be reworked. Design for test (DFT) decides, while the layout and the housing are still soft, where the probes land, where the pressure goes in, and which faults the production line will catch before the customer does. This module covers electrical test from in-circuit to boundary scan, mechanical test from leak to function, and the arithmetic of coverage and escapes.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish the tests that validate a design (EVT, DVT) from the tests that catch manufacturing defects (PVT and production), and place each in the right phase.
  • Select among in-circuit test, flying probe, boundary scan, functional test, optical, and X-ray inspection by the defects each finds and the access each needs.
  • Specify test points, probe clearances, tooling holes, and board support so that a bed-of-nails fixture can be built and will work.
  • Estimate test coverage and the escapes that follow from the nets and faults left untested.
  • Design mechanical assemblies for leak and pressure testing, including ports, seal-offs, and test volume.
  • Plan the test fixture alongside the product so that datums, access, and sequence are shared.

Why this matters

On 7 January 2013 a lithium-ion battery in the auxiliary power unit of a Boeing 787 caught fire on the ground in Boston. The National Transportation Safety Board's probable cause was "an internal short circuit within a cell of the APU lithium-ion battery, which led to thermal runaway that cascaded to adjacent cells", resulting from "Boeing's failure to incorporate design requirements to mitigate the most severe effects of an internal short circuit within an APU battery cell and the FAA's failure to identify this design deficiency during the type design certification process".[7] The report's findings read as a design-for-test post-mortem. The safety assessment assumed that an internal short in one cell would be limited to that cell venting; the cell manufacturer "did not test the battery under the most severe conditions possible in service"; the possibility of cascading thermal runaway "was not considered"; and as a result the certification engineers did not require a thermal-runaway test.[7] Investigators also found manufacturing conditions at the cell plant, including foreign object debris generated during welding, and an inspection process that could not reliably detect the defects that could cause an internal short.[7]

Two lessons transfer to any product. A test only validates the assumption it was designed around; if the worst credible case is not in the test plan, the test passes and the product does not. And inspection is itself a process with a detection capability; a defect the inspection cannot see is a defect that ships. Both lessons apply as much to a bed-of-nails fixture on a controller board or a leak test on a valve body as to an aircraft battery.

The 1996 Ariane 5 maiden flight makes the same point from the software side: the inquiry board found that the development programme "did not include adequate analysis and testing of the inertial reference system or of the complete flight control system, which could have detected the potential failure", and that the equipment and system tests "were not sufficiently representative".[8] Representative test conditions are a design deliverable.

Test strategy through the phases

Module 1 gave the builds; each one tests something different, and DFT has to serve all of them.

  • EVT tests ask whether the engineering works: bench tests of functions, components, and margins, run by engineers with instruments. They need access to signals and adjustments that production units may not expose; plan how that access is removed or disabled later.
  • DVT tests ask whether the complete design meets its requirements: performance, durability, environmental, and pre-compliance testing on production-intent parts.[9] These validate the design once. Their conditions must include the most severe credible case, which is the 787 lesson.
  • PVT and production tests ask a narrower question of every unit: was this one built correctly? They do not re-validate the design. They catch manufacturing defects (a missing part, a bridged joint, a leaking seal, a mis-wired connector) fast, repeatably, in a fixture, by an operator. This is the test that DFT designs for, and it has to exist before PVT, not after.

Production test is organised as a hierarchy: incoming inspection of parts, in-process inspection during assembly (optical inspection after reflow, for instance), sub-assembly test where a fault is still cheap to find, and final functional test. The design decision is where to put the sub-assembly tests. A board tested before it goes into a sealed housing is reworked at the bench; the same fault found at final test means opening the housing and, often, scrapping the seal.

In-circuit test deserves a closer look because it drives the most layout rules. The fixture is a plate of spring-loaded pins aligned to test points on the board; pressing the board onto the pins makes contact with every test point at once, and the tester measures resistance, capacitance, and continuity net by net.[1] Its limitations are also the design rules: limited access on dense boards, difficulty with high-frequency designs, potential bottlenecks in production, and fixture design and programming effort.[1] ICT finds the manufacturing defect and names the component; functional test finds that something is wrong. Most lines want both, and a board with good ICT coverage spends far less time at the functional station being diagnosed.

Boundary scan reaches where probes cannot. IEEE Std 1149.1 defines a Test Access Port of four signals (TDI, TMS, TCK, TDO) plus an optional reset, and a boundary-scan cell on each pin of a compliant device that passes data transparently in normal operation and can capture and shift it in test mode.[3],[4] Chained through several devices, it tests the interconnects between them for opens and shorts with no test points at all, which is the answer for nets under BGAs. The 2013 revision added features for power management and initialisation.[4] The design cost is small: choose scan-capable parts, chain them, and bring the TAP to a connector or five test points.

Test points and fixture access

The bed-of-nails fixture is a mechanical assembly, and it has dimensional rules. The following are one contract manufacturer's published guidelines and will differ in detail at yours.[2]

  • Test point size. 1.0 mm diameter gives the most reliable contact; 0.9 mm is the minimum recommended on the top side and 0.7 mm on the bottom; 0.6 mm is permitted on the bottom only. Test points must be free of solder mask, and the mask must not be tangent to them.
  • Probe pitch. The spacing between adjacent probes sets fixture cost and reliability: 2.54 mm (100 mil) pitch is the baseline (cost factor 1, most reliable); 1.905 mm (75 mil) roughly doubles the fixture cost; 1.27 mm (50 mil) roughly quadruples it and is the least reliable.
  • Clearance to components. A probe needs room beside components: 0.7 mm from parts under 2 mm tall, 1.91 mm from parts over 4 mm tall, 0.9 mm from the board or hole edge, and 3 mm from tooling holes. Leave a 5.08 mm gap on all four sides of a BGA.
  • Tooling holes. Two or three, preferably 2.7 mm diameter at ±0.05 mm, located to ±0.1 mm, at least 3 mm from the board edge, with a 0.3 mm solder-mask-free ring.
  • Support and rails. The board must be supported against probe force; the guideline calls for support about every 150 mm. Keep a 3 mm strip along the edges clear for handling and clamps.
  • Coverage rule. "At least one probable point for each network", and four test points on each power supply net so the tester can source current (under 1 A per point).

For flying probe, the same source gives the clearance a probe needs beside a component of height H as L = 0.29 × H + 0.7 mm (about 1.86 mm beside a 4 mm part), test points as small as 6 mil with 20 mil recommended, and a 2.8 mm allowance between probe and sensor.[2] Flying probe removes the fixture cost and is the usual choice for prototypes and low volume; ICT wins when volume amortises the fixture and cycle time matters.

Test coverage and escapes

Coverage is the fraction of what could go wrong that the test can detect. For ICT it is usually stated as the fraction of nets with a test point, or more carefully as the fraction of possible faults (opens, shorts, wrong values, missing parts) the program can catch. An escape is a defect that passes the test and moves downstream, where it costs more at every step (Module 0). The two are connected by simple arithmetic that is worth doing early.

Suppose a board has 2,000 solder joints and the process runs at a defect rate of 200 defects per million opportunities (DPMO). The expected number of defects per board is 2,000 × 200 × 10−6 = 0.4. If ICT covers 90 percent of the joints, 0.04 defects per board escape ICT; at 20,000 boards a year that is 800 defective boards reaching functional test. If functional test catches 80 percent of them, 160 reach the customer. The numbers are illustrative; the shape is general. Every net you cannot probe, and every fault type the test cannot see, is a fraction of the process defect rate that ships. Boundary scan on the nets under the BGAs, X-ray on the hidden joints, and a functional test that exercises the untested nets are how the fraction is driven down.

Set the coverage target before layout, because layout is where coverage is won or lost. The published guideline of one test point per net is the minimum for full ICT coverage;[2] every net that ends up without one is a conscious decision to test it some other way, or not at all, and the decision should be written down.

Testability of mechanical assemblies

Mechanical products are tested too, and the same logic applies: decide at architecture what the production test is, and design the parts to allow it.

Leak and pressure testing

Anything that holds fluid or gas gets a leak test, and two standard practices cover most cases. ASTM E2930 describes the pressure decay method: the vessel is pressurised, and the leak rate is determined from the change of mass inside, computed from measured pressure, temperature, and time using the ideal gas relationship; the practice does not apply to deformable vessels.[5] ASTM E1003 describes hydrostatic leak testing: components such as piping, valves, and containers are pressurised internally with a liquid; it is useful for quality control of items designed for internal pressure and expected to retain liquids, and is not sensitive enough where toxic or explosive gas is to be retained.[6]

The design consequences of pressure decay follow from the physics. For a gas at pressure P in a test volume V, a small leak Q over time t produces a pressure drop of roughly Δp ≈ P · Q · t / V. So a smaller test volume gives a larger, faster signal for the same leak; a stiff vessel is needed because a deforming wall changes V and masks the leak; and temperature must be stable because a small warming looks like a leak in reverse. Design for it by keeping the tested volume small (test the sub-assembly, not the whole product; block off large dead volumes with the fixture), by providing a test port that can be sealed with a quick connector and that becomes a plugged or capped feature in the finished product, by giving the fixture a clamping surface and a datum, and by choosing materials and geometry that do not creep under test pressure.

Functional checks

Mechanical functional tests (a valve strokes fully, a latch holds a load, a motor draws its expected current, a fan delivers flow) need the same three things as an electrical test: access to apply the stimulus, access to measure the response, and a repeatable way to hold the part. Design them in: a drive feature that a fixture can engage, a port where a pressure or flow gauge can attach, a flat where a load cell can push, and a way to reach the connector before the cover goes on. A product whose only functional test is "switch it on and see" has no diagnosis when it fails.

Designing the fixture alongside the product

Fixtures designed after the product are designed around obstacles. Fixtures designed with it share its datums and its sequence. The habits:

  1. Same datums. The fixture locates the board on the tooling holes and the housing on the mounting features that the assembly uses (Module 3). A fixture that locates on a cosmetic surface tests a slightly different part every time.
  2. Reserve the zones. Probe clearances, tooling holes, clamp areas, the 3 mm edge strip, and the leak-test port are keep-outs on the layout and the housing from the first concept.
  3. Test before you seal. Put the sub-assembly test at the point in the assembly sequence (Module 10) just before the fault becomes expensive to reach.
  4. Build the fixture during EVT. A printed or machined bench fixture (Module 8) used on the first thirty units teaches the production fixture what it needs to hold, reach, and measure, and it exists before the DVT tools are cut (Module 1).
  5. Prove the test can see the fault. Seed known defects (a lifted pin, a missing part, a pinhole leak) and confirm the test catches them. This is the inspection-capability lesson of the 787: an inspection that has never been shown to detect the defect is an assumption, not a test.

Real-world example: the 787 battery certification tests

The NTSB report on the Boston fire (NTSB/AIR-14/01, adopted 21 November 2014) is a study in test design.[7] The battery had eight cells. The safety assessment used for certification assumed that an internal short circuit within a cell would be limited to venting of that cell; the possibility that thermal runaway in one cell could cascade to the others "was not considered", and so the certification engineers did not require a thermal-runaway test.[7] The cell manufacturer "did not test the battery under the most severe conditions possible in service".[7] After the event, independent testing induced internal shorts in single cells by indentation, nail penetration, and heating, and in every case the cell entered thermal runaway.[7]

On the manufacturing side, investigators visiting the cell plant found conditions including foreign object debris generated during cell welding operations and an inspection process that could not reliably detect defects that could lead to an internal short circuit; oversight of the supply chain did not ensure that the cell manufacturing conditions were adequate.[7] The NTSB's safety issues included both "cell internal short circuiting and the potential for thermal runaway" and "cell manufacturing defects and oversight of cell manufacturing processes".[7]

Three transferable lessons. First, list the failure modes before you list the tests, and make sure the worst credible one has a test; the absent test here was the one for propagation between cells. Second, treat inspection as a process with a measurable detection capability, and prove it on seeded defects. Third, test conditions must be representative of service, which is the same finding the Ariane 5 board made about its simulations two decades earlier.[8] None of this is specific to aviation. A leak test that never sees a pinhole, an ICT program that skips the nets under a BGA, and a functional test that runs at room temperature when the product lives in a boiler cupboard all share the same flaw.

Common mistakes

  1. Laying out the board, then asking for a fixture. Consequence: test points squeezed in where they fit, nets untestable, a fixture at fine pitch that costs four times as much. Fix: reserve test points, tooling holes, and clearances before routing.
  2. Test points with solder mask over or tangent to them. Consequence: intermittent contact and false failures. Fix: mask-free pads with clearance, 1.0 mm where possible.
  3. No test point on a net because it is under a BGA. Consequence: an untested interconnect. Fix: boundary scan through the BGA and its neighbours, or a via brought out to a pad.
  4. Functional test as the only test. Consequence: failures with no diagnosis, long debug at the station, and latent defects that pass. Fix: ICT or flying probe for structural faults, functional test for behaviour.
  5. Testing after the product is sealed. Consequence: every rework opens a housing and scraps a seal. Fix: sub-assembly test before the sealing step.
  6. Leak testing the whole product volume. Consequence: a small leak gives a tiny pressure drop; the test is long or insensitive. Fix: test the pressure-bearing sub-assembly; block dead volume; keep the vessel stiff and the temperature stable.
  7. Assuming the inspection sees the defect. Consequence: defects ship with a passed inspection record. Fix: seed known defects and measure detection.
  8. Test conditions that are not the worst credible case. Consequence: the design passes and the product fails. Fix: derive test conditions from the failure mode list, not from convenience.

Worked exercise: coverage, escapes, and a leak test

This is an illustrative example with invented but realistic numbers, not a real program.

A controller board has 480 nets and 2,000 solder joints. Twenty nets exist only under a BGA and cannot be probed. Thirty more nets are in an area where the test points would need 1.27 mm pitch. The assembly process runs at 200 DPMO on solder joints. Annual volume is 20,000 boards. The board sits in a housing that is leak tested by pressure decay at a gauge pressure of 300 kPa; the housing's internal volume is 500 cm³; the acceptance limit is a leak of 1 cm³/min at test pressure; the pressure sensor resolves 20 Pa.

Tasks. (a) Compute ICT net coverage if the BGA nets and the dense nets are left unprobed, and then if boundary scan covers the BGA nets. (b) Estimate escapes from ICT per year in each case, assuming joint coverage equals net coverage. (c) Compare the fixture cost factor of probing the dense nets at 1.27 mm pitch against leaving them to functional test. (d) Compute the pressure drop for the limit leak over a 30 s hold, and what a 200 cm³ test volume would do to the test time.

Show the worked solution

(a) Coverage. Without the BGA and dense nets: (480 − 20 − 30) / 480 = 430 / 480 = 89.6 percent. With boundary scan covering the 20 BGA nets: 450 / 480 = 93.75 percent.

(b) Escapes. Expected defects per board = 2,000 × 200 × 10−6 = 0.40. Uncovered share 10.4 percent: escapes = 0.40 × 0.104 = 0.042 per board, or about 830 defective boards per year reaching the next station. With boundary scan, uncovered share 6.25 percent: 0.025 per board, about 500 per year. If functional test then catches 80 percent, the field sees about 166 or 100 boards respectively. Each of those is a warranty return that started as a layout decision.

(c) Fixture cost. Probing the 30 dense nets at 1.27 mm pitch raises the fixture cost factor from about 1 to about 4 for that region (and the reliability falls). Alternatives: move the test points for those nets to a less dense area by adding vias to pads at 2.54 mm pitch, which costs layout time and a little board area but keeps the fixture cheap and reliable; or leave them to functional test and accept the escape arithmetic in (b) for 30 nets. The layout change is almost always the right answer when it is made before routing is complete.

(d) Leak test. Δp ≈ P · Q · t / V. Use absolute pressure for the ideal gas relationship: about 300 + 101 = 401 kPa. Q · t = 1 cm³/min × 0.5 min = 0.5 cm³. Δp ≈ 401 kPa × 0.5 / 500 = 0.40 kPa = 400 Pa, twenty times the 20 Pa resolution, so the 30 s hold detects the limit leak with margin. With a 200 cm³ test volume (a fixture insert filling the dead space), the same leak gives 1,000 Pa in 30 s, or 400 Pa in 12 s; the hold can be shortened to about 12 s for the same margin, which at 20,000 units a year is 100 hours of test time saved. The stabilisation time before the hold (temperature settling after fill) does not shrink with volume and is often the larger part of the cycle; measure it on the first fixture rather than assuming it.

Key takeaways

  • DVT validates the design once; production test asks every unit whether it was built correctly. Design for the second one, and make sure the first one includes the worst credible case.
  • Use the method that finds the fault: AOI and X-ray for joints, ICT or flying probe for structure and values, boundary scan for nets probes cannot reach, functional test for behaviour.
  • The fixture is a mechanical assembly with its own rules: 1.0 mm pads, 2.54 mm pitch where possible, height-dependent clearances, precise tooling holes, support, and a clear edge strip. Reserve them before routing.
  • Coverage is decided in layout. Escapes are the untested share of the defect rate, and they cost more at every downstream step.
  • For leak testing, keep the test volume small, the vessel stiff, and the temperature stable; provide a port and a clamping datum; test the sub-assembly before it is buried.
  • Design the fixture with the product, on the same datums, and build a bench version during EVT.
  • Prove the test can see the fault. Seed defects. An inspection that has never caught the defect is an assumption.
