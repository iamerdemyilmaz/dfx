Module 16

Capstone: a full DFx review

This module is the exam. It specifies one product completely, a portable 12 V DC tyre inflator, and takes it through the four reviews this course has taught: design for manufacturing, for assembly, for test, and for service. The output is what a real review produces: a scored findings list with dispositions, and a redesigned bill of materials with the part count, fastener count, and assembly time before and after. Read the baseline design first, write your own findings, then compare them with the review below. The worked exercise and quiz ask you to finish the parts the review leaves open.

The product, its parts list, its dimensions, and every cost and time figure in this module are illustrative. This is not a real program. Every design rule applied is cited to the module and source that introduced it.

Learning objectives

  • Conduct a DFM review of moulded, die-cast, machined, sheet metal, and PCB parts against cited quantitative rules.
  • Apply the Boothroyd Dewhurst minimum-part questions to a full assembly and compute a DFA index before and after redesign.
  • Specify the test access, test points, and test sequence a product needs before its housing closes.
  • Identify field-replaceable units, wear parts, and the compliance constraints that reach into material and finish choices.
  • Score findings by severity and likelihood, disposition each one, and produce a redesigned BOM with quantified changes.

Why this matters

A DFx review is where the course's rules stop being rules and start being decisions. The results that Boothroyd Dewhurst publishes for its customers show the scale a complete review can reach on a real product: an IDEXX Laboratories analyser door went from 183 parts to 31, from 63 fasteners to none, and from 45 minutes of assembly to 11, its DFA index rising from 3.8 to 35.8.[1] Those are vendor-published figures and should be read as such, but the mechanism behind them is public and reproducible: ask of every part whether it must move, must be a different material, or must be separate for assembly or service, and remove what fails all three.[1]

A smaller, fully worked case shows what a review looks like at the scale of a single enclosure. An L3Harris engineer's DFMA Forum walkthrough analysed an electronics enclosure of 11 parts with a theoretical minimum of 3, an estimated assembly time of 87.6 seconds, and a DFA index of 11.76; replacing four cover screws with a snap-fit cover raised the index to 17.63 and removed about 29 seconds and $0.95 of process cost per unit.[2] The inflator below is a bigger version of that enclosure: more processes, more fasteners, a PCBA, a pressurised subassembly, and a service story. The method is the same.

The order matters. DFM findings change parts; DFA findings remove them; DFT findings add access; DFS findings decide which joints must reopen. Run them in that order and each review works on a design that the previous one has already simplified.

Scoring method

Each finding gets a severity from 1 to 3 and a likelihood from 1 to 3; the score is their product. Severity 3 means the part cannot be made as drawn, or a safety or compliance failure; 2 means a yield, quality, or field-return risk; 1 means cost only. Likelihood 3 means it will happen on every part; 2 on a meaningful fraction; 1 rarely. Scores of 6 and above are fixed before tooling is cut. Scores of 3 and 4 are fixed if the change costs nothing at this stage. Scores of 1 and 2 are recorded and revisited at the next cost-down. Every finding gets a disposition (accept, negotiate, reject) and an owner, as in Module 14.

DFM review

Take the parts in BOM order and check each one against the rules table of the module for its process. The moulded housings get the Module 6 checks (wall, draft, ribs, bosses, undercuts, tolerances, material); the die-cast head gets Module 7 (wall, draft, corner radii, machining allowance); the machined parts get Module 4 (tolerances, thread depth, corner radii); the sheet metal gets Module 5 (bend radius, flange, hole-to-bend); the PCBA gets Module 9 (fabrication tier rules, current capacity, fiducials); and every part gets Module 13 (finish specified completely, galvanic pairs, service temperature). Write the finding, cite the rule, score it, and propose a fix. Do not stop at the first finding on a part; the housings alone produced six.

DFA review

Apply the three questions to every part: does it move relative to the parts already assembled; must it be a different material; must it be separate for assembly, service, or adjustment?[1] The theoretical minimum for the inflator comes to 13: two housing halves (one would do for the count, but service requires opening, so two are justified), a compressor block, a piston, a connecting rod, a crank, a bearing, a motor, a PCBA with display and keypad treated as one because they could be one assembly, a power cord, a hose, a fitting in brass, a piston seal in PTFE, and a set of reed valves in spring steel. Fasteners, brackets, shields, windows, lenses, feet, gaskets, clips, and grommets all fail all three questions.

Assembly time is estimated with the simplified method of Module 10: a theoretical minimum time of about 3 seconds per part (Boothroyd Dewhurst use 2.93 s), against an estimated actual time built from handling and insertion allowances, with screws carrying a penalty for tool acquisition, alignment, and driving.[2][12] The estimate below uses round allowances, stated so you can redo it with your own line's times.

DFS review: service, reliability, and compliance

The service review starts from a short list: what wears, what fails, what the user will break, and what a regulator will ask for. On this product the wear part is the piston cup seal; the likely failures are the motor brushes, the fuse, and the cord at its strain relief; the user will lose the chuck and the adaptor nozzles; and the regulator will ask for materials declarations and the CE technical file. Each of those items gets a path: replaceable by the user, by a technician, or not at all, and each path is checked against the fastener count and the adhesives the DFA review left behind. The reliability items come from Module 12's derating and thermal rules; the compliance items reach back into the DFM review, because the finish specification on a steel bracket is where RoHS actually lives.

The review took about a day of one engineer's time with the drawings, plus a two-hour meeting with electrical, test, and sourcing to agree the dispositions. That is the normal cost of a first DFx review on a product of this size, and it is the cheapest day on the program: every finding in Table 8 would have been a tool change, a board respin, a fixture rebuild, or a compliance failure if found at DVT.

Thirty-eight findings in total: 17 DFM, 9 DFA, 6 DFT, 6 DFS. Eighteen score 6 or more. Two were negotiated rather than accepted (DFM-12, DFM-16) and none rejected, which is typical of a first review on a design that has not seen a supplier yet; later reviews reject more, because the cheap changes have been made.

The cost side, in the terms of Module 15 and with illustrative rates: the housing tool loses a side action (about $1,000 to 1,500 saved on tooling and a few seconds of cycle) and gains cradle, slot, and lip features (several hundred dollars of additional cavity work).[4] The steel bracket, its plating, and 16 screws leave the BOM. The cylinder machined from bar and the valve plate leave; the die casting grows and needs honing, which was already needed. About 290 seconds of assembly time per unit leave; at an illustrative loaded labour rate of $0.50 per minute that is about $2.40 per unit, or about $49,000 per year at 20,000 units, against a one-time cavity change measured in low thousands. The redesign also makes the product testable, serviceable, and RoHS-compliant, none of which the baseline was. Those do not appear in a cost model, and they are the reason the review exists.

Real-world example: what a finished DFA review reports

The inflator review is invented. The reporting format is not. The L3Harris walkthrough at the 2019 DFMA Forum lays out an electronics enclosure exactly the way Tables 4, 5, 10, and 11 do: a parts list with a theoretical-minimum judgement against each item, a handling and insertion time for each, a total (87.6 seconds for 11 parts), the index (11.76), a proposed change (a snap-fit cover in place of four screws), and the recomputed result (17.63, about 29 seconds and $0.95 saved).[2] A 2013 Forum paper from Acorn Product Development shows the same method by hand, with the assembly efficiency written as the minimum part count times a base time of about 3 seconds divided by the estimated time, and with penalties for fastener type and for the extra passes a screw pattern needs.[12] At the other end of the scale, the IDEXX door case shows what several rounds of the same reasoning did on a complex assembly.[1] The format is the deliverable: a list a supplier can quote, a manager can read, and the next engineer can audit.

Common mistakes in running a review

  1. Reviewing DFA before DFM. Consequence: assembly time is optimised on parts that will change shape. Fix: DFM first, then DFA, DFT, DFS.
  2. Findings without scores. Consequence: forty items of equal weight, none fixed. Fix: severity × likelihood, a threshold, and a gate for each.
  3. Findings without dispositions. Consequence: nobody knows what was decided. Fix: accept, negotiate, or reject, with an owner and a reason (Module 14).
  4. Counting parts but not fasteners, drives, adhesives, and solder joints. Consequence: the assembly time estimate misses the operations that cost most. Fix: count every operation, not just every part number.
  5. Removing a fastener a service technician needs. Consequence: a wear part behind a glued or welded joint. Fix: run DFS on the DFA result and keep openable joints where the wear parts are.
  6. Test as an afterthought. Consequence: a subassembly that can only be tested through the finished product. Fix: design the fixture datums and the test sequence with the part (Module 11).
  7. Compliance as a checkbox. Consequence: a passivate that fails RoHS discovered at PVT. Fix: material and finish specifications carry the compliance requirement on the drawing.
  8. Claiming precision the model does not have. Consequence: a "$2.40 saving" argued to the cent. Fix: state allowances and rates as assumptions and report ranges; the direction and order of magnitude are what the decision needs.

Worked exercise: finish the review

Four items the review left for you. Show your working.

  1. Recompute the IPC-2221 trace width in DFM-14 for 10 A at a 20 °C rise on 1 oz (35 µm, 1.378 mil) and 2 oz copper. Then check whether the redesign's 3.0 mm trace at 2 oz meets a 20 °C rise.
  2. The redesigned compressor block has a 20 mm deep internal cavity. Compute the NADCA draft angle with the formula in DFM-07 and compare it with the 1° the review specified.
  3. If the redesign kept the sheet metal bracket instead of adopting DFA-03, give the minimum hole-to-bend distance, flange length, and inside bend radius for 2.0 mm steel, and estimate the forming time for its four bends at 30 s each.
  4. Suppose the line's real allowances are 12 s per screw and 4 s per other part, with everything else as in Tables 4 and 11. Recompute both assembly times and both DFA indices.
Show the worked solution

1. Trace width. Rearranging I = 0.048 · ΔT0.44 · A0.725: A = (I ÷ (0.048 · ΔT0.44))1/0.725. With ΔT = 20 °C, 200.44 = 3.74, so 0.048 × 3.74 = 0.179. Then 10 ÷ 0.179 = 55.7, and 55.71.379 = 256 square mils. At 1 oz (1.378 mil thick) the width is 256 ÷ 1.378 = 186 mil = 4.7 mm. At 2 oz (2.756 mil) it is 93 mil = 2.4 mm. The redesign's 3.0 mm at 2 oz is 118 mil wide, A = 118 × 2.756 = 325 square mils, and I = 0.048 × 3.74 × 3250.725 = 0.179 × 66.2 = 11.9 A at a 20 °C rise, so it carries 10 A with margin. This is the conservative IPC-2221 estimate; IPC-2152 would allow less copper (Module 9).

2. Die-cast draft. D = √L ÷ C = √20 ÷ 6.00 = 4.47 ÷ 6.00 = 0.75°. The review's 1° is above the formula minimum and below NADCA's general 2° note for 25 mm and deeper features; it is a reasonable specification. Say so on the drawing: "1° minimum inside draft; die caster may increase".

3. Bracket rules. For t = 2.0 mm: hole to bend ≥ 2.5 × 2.0 = 5.0 mm; flange ≥ 4 × 2.0 = 8.0 mm; inside bend radius ≥ 1 × 2.0 = 2.0 mm. Forming time 4 bends × 30 s = 120 s per bracket, which at a $90 per hour brake rate (Module 15) is $3.00 of forming per unit before setup, material, plating, and the ten screws it needs. That number is why DFA-03 wins.

4. Revised allowances. Baseline: 26 × 12 = 312; 35 × 4 = 140; plus 30 + 32 + 48 + 30 = 140; total 592 s; index 13 × 2.93 ÷ 592 = 0.064. Redesign: 9 × 12 = 108; 29 × 4 = 116; plus 15 + 0 + 6 + 10 = 31; total 255 s; index 13 × 2.93 ÷ 255 = 0.149. The redesign's time barely moves because it has few screws, which is where the allowance changed most; the baseline gets worse. The ranking and the order of magnitude did not change, which is what a robust review looks like.

Quiz

Eight questions. Score 70 % or more to mark this module complete on this device.

1. In what order does this module recommend running the four reviews, and why?

DFM changes parts, DFA removes them, DFT adds access, DFS decides which joints must reopen.

2. The redesign has a theoretical minimum of 13 parts and an estimated assembly time of 257 s. What is its DFA index (as a decimal, using 2.93 s per part)?

13 × 2.93 ÷ 257 = 0.148.

3. Why did finding DFM-11 (plating specified only as "zinc") score 9, the highest possible?

Severity 3 (compliance) times likelihood 3 (every part) = 9. Specify class, thickness, and a trivalent passivate.

4. Using IPC-2221 (I = 0.048 · ΔT^0.44 · A^0.725), about how wide in mm must a 1 oz (1.378 mil) external trace be to carry 10 A at a 20 °C rise?

A ≈ 256 square mils; 256 ÷ 1.378 = 186 mil ≈ 4.7 mm.

5. Which baseline part fails all three minimum-part questions?

The bracket does not move, need not be a different material from the housing's function, and is not needed for assembly or service once a cradle is moulded in. The piston moves, the seal and fitting need their materials.

6. Finding DFT-04 asks for the compressor block to be run and leak tested as a subassembly. The main reason is

Test subassemblies before they are buried; the cost of finding a fault rises with every operation after it was introduced.

7. Using D = √L ÷ C with C = 6.00 for an internal aluminium die-cast wall, what draft angle in degrees does the formula give for a 20 mm deep cavity?

√20 = 4.47; 4.47 ÷ 6.00 = 0.75°.

8. In the scoring method, a finding with severity 2 and likelihood 3

Score = severity × likelihood = 6; the threshold for fixing before tooling is 6.

  2. 0.148.
  3. d. Possible hexavalent chromate against RoHS.
  4. About 4.7 mm.
  5. a. The bracket.

Key takeaways

  • A review is a list: finding, rule and source, score, disposition, owner, gate. Anything less is a conversation.
  • Run DFM, then DFA, then DFT, then DFS. Each works on what the previous one left.
  • Ask the three questions of every part. The inflator's 61 parts had a theoretical minimum of 13; the redesign reached 38 and did not need to reach 13.
  • Count operations, not part numbers: fasteners, drive types, adhesives, solder joints, and reorientations are where assembly time lives.
  • Test the subassembly before it is buried, and give the fixture the same datums the housing uses.
  • Service and compliance findings can outrank everything else: an unspecified passivate scored higher than any moulding rule.
  • State your allowances and rates. The redesign's value is in its direction and order of magnitude, not in the second decimal.
