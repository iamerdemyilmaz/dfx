Module 7

DFM for casting and forging

Casting and forging make metal parts by shaping them hot, in a mould or between dies, instead of cutting them cold. They can consolidate many machined or stamped parts into one and put metal only where it is needed. In return they ask for draft, uniform walls, generous radii, a tolerance budget that respects the parting line, and honesty about what is inside the metal.

Learning objectives

  • Choose between high-pressure die casting, investment casting, sand casting, and forging on the basis of volume, tolerance, alloy, and geometry.
  • Calculate die-casting draft with the NADCA formula and apply the standard and precision linear tolerances.
  • Specify wall thickness, fillets, and corner radii for castings and forgings, and explain why inside and outside draft differ.
  • Place datums and critical dimensions in one die half and add machining allowance where a cast surface cannot serve.
  • Explain where porosity, parting-line shift, and internal defects come from and how design and inspection address them.
  • Decide, with a break-even, when a casting beats a machined part and when it does not.

Why this matters

On 19 July 1989 the stage 1 fan disk of the tail engine of a DC-10 burst in flight; the debris cut all three of the aircraft's hydraulic systems, and the crew's crash landing at Sioux City, Iowa, was survived by 185 of the 296 people on board. The disk was a titanium forging. The FAA's lessons-learned record states that "the fan disk failure was caused by a fatigue crack that initiated from a metallurgical defect located on the surface of the disk bore", and that the defect "formed during the initial manufacture of the titanium alloy material and was not detected by inspections performed during the manufacturing process" in 1971.[8] The National Transportation Safety Board's probable cause was the inadequate consideration of human-factors limitations in the inspection and quality-control procedures at the overhaul facility, which "resulted in the failure to detect a fatigue crack originating from a previously undetected metallurgical defect".[8] At the last fluorescent penetrant inspection, 760 cycles before the accident, the surface crack is estimated to have been about 12 mm (0.5 in) long, and it was not found.[8] The industry response was to change how the material was made and inspected: the double vacuum arc remelt process was judged "inadequate to consistently produce defect-free parts" and gave way to triple melting, and an airworthiness directive required ultrasonic inspection of the affected disks.[8]

Few readers will design fan disks. Everyone who specifies a casting or forging inherits the same fact: the process puts material where you cannot see it, and defects in the melt, the fill, or the flow become defects inside the part. A die casting traps gas as porosity; a sand casting has inclusions and shrinkage cavities; a forging has flow lines and, occasionally, whatever was in the billet. Design for these processes therefore includes designing the inspection: which surfaces will be machined and how far, where porosity is tolerable and where it is not, and what the foundry or forge will check before the part ships.

The positive side of the same coin is consolidation. The most public recent example is Tesla's move to cast the rear underbody of the Model Y as one or two large aluminium castings in place of about 70 stamped and joined parts, described in the next sections. Casting removes joints, fasteners, and assembly steps; the price is a set of rules about walls, draft, and tolerances that this module lays out.

The casting family, and forging

Four processes cover most engineered metal parts that are not machined from stock or formed from sheet.

  • High-pressure die casting (HPDC). Molten aluminium, zinc, or magnesium injected under high pressure into a hardened steel die. Fast cycles, thin walls, good surface, tight tolerances for a casting; expensive dies, so high volume. Gas trapped in the fast fill leaves porosity.
  • Investment casting. A wax pattern is coated in ceramic, the wax is melted out, and metal is poured into the shell. Almost any alloy, fine detail, good finish, no parting line on the part; slow and labour-intensive, so low to medium volume.
  • Sand casting. Metal poured into a sand mould made around a pattern. Any size, any alloy, cheap tooling; rough surface, generous tolerances, machining on every functional face.
  • Forging. Hot or cold metal deformed between dies. No melting, so a wrought grain structure that follows the part shape and the best fatigue properties of the four; dies are expensive and the shapes are limited to what metal will flow into.

The die casting industry's own standard states the volume logic plainly: at low volumes investment casting can reach precision tolerances economically, while at higher volumes die casting is "the clear choice"; powder metallurgy offers accuracy but not die casting's thin walls and detail; and plastics can match the shape but not rigidity, creep resistance, or strength at temperature.[1] Module 2 gives the break-even arithmetic; this module gives the geometry rules for each process.

High-pressure die casting

Walls, fillets, and uniformity

Typical die-cast wall thickness ranges from 1.0 to 5.1 mm (0.040 to 0.200 in) depending on alloy, configuration, size, and application; smaller castings can be cast with walls as thin as 0.5 mm with die caster consultation.[1] NADCA's illustration of good practice has sharp corners eliminated and proper draft and radii provided "to maximize the potential die life and to aid in filling the die cavity completely under high production cycle speeds".[1] The reasoning is the same as for moulding (Module 6): thick sections cool slowly and shrink last, and in a die casting the last-to-solidify region is where gas and shrinkage porosity concentrate. Keep walls uniform, core out heavy sections, and put fillets at every intersection.

Draft

Every surface perpendicular to the parting line needs draft so the casting releases from the die. NADCA's standard draft is not a fixed angle. It is computed from the depth of the feature and a constant that depends on alloy and on whether the surface is inside or outside:[1]

D = √L / C, where D is the draft distance, L the depth of the feature from the parting line, and C a constant from the table below. The draft angle follows from tan θ = D / L. Because of the square root, the angle decreases as the feature gets deeper.

Two features of the table carry the physics. Inside walls need twice the draft of outside walls "because as the alloy solidifies it shrinks onto the die features that form inside surfaces (usually located in the ejector half) and away from features that form outside surfaces".[1] And the constant is smaller for alloys that grip the die more. In practice draft is specified by a general note with exceptions; NADCA's example is that an aluminium casting whose features are at least 25 mm deep can be covered by "2° minimum draft on inside surfaces and 1° minimum on outside surfaces".[1] Less draft than the formula gives is available as a precision tolerance, at a price.[1]

Tolerances and the parting line

NADCA publishes two tolerance levels. Standard tolerances represent "normal casting production practice at the most economical level"; precision tolerances involve "extra precision in die construction and/or special control in production" and "should be specified only when and where necessary".[1] For linear dimensions formed in the same die half with no moving components, the standard tolerance is ±0.25 mm on the first 25.4 mm plus ±0.025 mm for each additional 25.4 mm; the precision tolerance is ±0.05 mm on the first 25.4 mm plus the same ±0.025 mm per additional 25.4 mm.[1] NADCA's own example: a 127 mm (5 in) aluminium dimension carries ±0.35 mm standard or ±0.15 mm precision.[1]

The qualifier matters more than the numbers. Linear tolerance "only applies to linear dimensions formed in the same die half with no moving components"; dimensions across the parting line or formed by slides must be increased for parting-line shift and slide movement.[1] This is the casting version of the rule from Modules 3 and 6: keep functional dimensions in one half of the tool. NADCA also notes the economic reason to avoid over-tolerancing: tighter tolerances mean die wear affects the part sooner and production runs are shorter between die maintenance, so "the objective is to have as much tolerance as possible without affecting form, fit and function".[1] Zinc, which creeps after casting, may need artificial ageing to hold critical dimensions.[1]

Porosity and ejector marks

High-pressure fill traps gas, and thick sections shrink after the skin freezes; both leave porosity inside the casting. The dense skin of a die casting is its best material, so machining deep into a die casting can open porosity that was harmlessly buried. Design consequence: keep machining stock on die castings thin, avoid machining into heavy sections, and place sealing surfaces and pressure-tight regions where the wall is uniform and the fill is good. Ask the die caster where the gate and overflows will be, because the last metal to fill is the most porous. Ejector pins leave shallow marks on the ejector-half surfaces; specify which surfaces are cosmetic and let the die caster put the pins elsewhere.

Investment casting

Investment casting reproduces a wax pattern closely, in almost any alloy, with a surface far better than sand. One foundry's published guidelines, attributed to Investment Casting Institute practice, give linear tolerances of ±0.25 mm on the first 25.4 mm and ±0.08 to 0.13 mm for each additional 25.4 mm; a wall-thickness tolerance of ±0.51 mm as a minimum absolute; flatness and straightness of about 0.13 mm per 25 mm; angularity of ±0.5° to ±2° depending on how the feature is supported; and a general surface finish of about 1.6 to 3.2 µm Ra (63 to 125 RMS).[4] Investment castings have no parting line on the part itself (the wax pattern's die has one), which is why complex undercut shapes that would need slides in a die casting are routine.

The process is slow and manual by comparison with die casting, and its wax and shell steps limit size. It earns its place at low to medium volume for complex shapes, for alloys that cannot be die cast (steels, superalloys, cobalt and titanium alloys), and where a near-net shape saves expensive machining. Minimum wall and feature sizes vary widely with alloy and foundry; ask yours rather than trusting a table.

Sand casting

Sand casting has the cheapest tooling and the widest size range, and the roughest results. Published guidance gives minimum wall thicknesses of about 2.5 mm (0.10 in) for aluminium and 3.3 mm (0.13 in) for magnesium and steel; a standard draft of 2° with a minimum of about 1° on external and internal features; a parting-line mismatch and flash allowance that should not exceed about 0.5 mm (0.020 in); an as-cast surface of 200 to 500 RMS; and a rule to avoid section thickness ratios above 2 to 1, tapering unavoidable transitions no steeper than 1 in 4.[2] Another guide gives draft of 1° to 2° on external walls, 2° to 3° on internal pockets, and 3° to 5° in deep cavities, and notes that machining allowances depend on alloy, casting size, and finish requirement and should be set with the foundry.[3]

Because the as-cast surface is rough and the tolerances generous, every sealing, locating, and bearing face on a sand casting is machined. Design the casting with that in mind: leave machining stock on those faces, provide cast locating pads or bosses for the first machining setup, and keep the machined faces in one plane where possible so that they can be finished in one pass.

Forging

Forging deforms solid metal between dies. Nothing melts, so the wrought structure is preserved and the grain flow follows the part shape, which is why forgings carry the highest fatigue and impact loads of the family. The Forging Industry Association's design guide gives the rules.[5]

  • Draft. Draft angles should be "the maximum allowable, consistent with functional, assembly and weight constraints". For ferrous forgings, draft angles below 5° "usually prohibit the use of hammers"; press dies with knock-out pins can produce forgings with little or no draft. Less draft is needed on the outside of a feature than on the inside, for the same reason as in casting. Heat-resistant alloys typically carry 7° to 10°.[5] Cold forgings need no draft.[5]
  • Fillets and corners. Interior corner (fillet) radii depend on rib height and alloy forgeability; sharp fillets obstruct metal flow and may require extra preform dies. For 25 mm high ribs the guide's representative values are: carbon steels, fillet 10 to 13 mm preferred and 6 mm minimum, corner 3 mm preferred and 1.5 mm minimum; stainless steels, fillet 6 to 13 mm preferred and 5 mm minimum, corner 5 mm preferred and 2.5 mm minimum; titanium alloys, fillet 13 to 16 mm preferred and 10 mm minimum, corner 6 mm preferred and 3 mm minimum.[5]
  • Parting line and balance. Forging cost is minimised and tolerances reduced when forging loads are balanced, eliminating side thrust. A part oriented so that it generates side thrust needs a counterlock in the die, which wears; rotating the part to balance the loads removes the counterlock but may mean features such as holes in bosses can no longer be forged and must be fully machined.[5] Flash forms where excess metal escapes at the parting line and is trimmed afterwards.[5]
  • Tolerances and datums. Features held to close tolerances should be formed in the same die member to avoid cross-die tolerance, and all datum targets and tooling points should be on features made in the same die half, preferably the upper die where there is less die contact and cooling.[5] Net-shape forging can hold remarkable tolerances on specific features (gear teeth up to 125 mm diameter forged to ±0.10 mm), but general forging tolerances are generous and appendices of the FIA guide give the industry values.[5] For rough-turned rolled rings, a tolerance of about ±1.6 mm and a 6.3 µm finish are practical.[5]

Machining allowances and datums

Castings and forgings are rarely finished parts. The near-net shape is machined where the function needs a flat, a bore, a thread, or a seal. Three rules keep that machining cheap.

  1. Leave stock only where you will machine, and enough of it to clean up the cast or forged surface under worst-case draft, mismatch, and tolerance. Stock that is too thin leaves an unmachined patch; too thick wastes time and, on a die casting, cuts into porosity.
  2. Put the first-operation datums on features in one die half, as both NADCA and the FIA guide advise, so the machining fixture locates on surfaces whose relationship the tool controls.[1],[5] Cast-in locating pads are cheap; a machining setup that locates on a drafted, mismatched surface is not.
  3. Machine related features in one setup. Bores that must be coaxial, faces that must be parallel, and a pattern of holes that must align with a mating part should all be cut without re-fixturing, exactly as in Module 4.

When casting beats machining

The comparison is the break-even of Module 2 with two additions. A casting or forging consolidates parts: internal passages, bosses, and ribs that would be several machined pieces or a welded fabrication become one part with no joints. And it changes the material: a die casting is a casting alloy with porosity, a forging has directional properties, and a machined part is uniform wrought stock. Casting wins when volume amortises the die, when the shape has internal features a cutter cannot reach, and when the tolerance budget can live with ±0.25 mm plus parting-line effects on most dimensions and machining on the few that cannot. Machining wins when volume is low, when every dimension is tight, when the design is still changing, and when the material properties of wrought stock are needed throughout. Forging wins when fatigue or impact loading is the driver and the shape is forgeable.

The Tesla case below is the extreme of consolidation at volume. Your version will be smaller: a machined block with cross-drilled passages that becomes a die casting with cored passages at 20,000 units a year, or a welded steel bracket that becomes a forging because it keeps failing in fatigue.

Real-world example: the Model Y rear underbody

Tesla's Q4 2019 shareholder update described the Model Y as having "basically two big high-pressure die-cast (HPDC) aluminium castings that are joined", with a plan to transition "to the rear underbody being a single-piece casting that also integrates the rear crash rails".[7] On the Q1 2020 earnings call Elon Musk said of the Model 3's stamped rear underbody: "When we get the big casting machine, it'll go from 70 parts to 1 with a significant reduction in capital expenditure on all the robots to put those parts together."[6] By January 2021 press reports of production vehicles showed the single-piece rear casting in place; the Model 3's rear underbody had been about 70 stamped metal parts, and the Model Y's was two large castings moving to one.[9]

Everything in this module is visible in that decision. Consolidation removes not only 69 parts but the joining operations and the robots that performed them, which is the capital-expenditure argument Musk made. The casting is high-pressure die cast aluminium, so it follows the die-casting rules: uniform walls, draft on every surface, tolerances that depend on which die half forms a feature, and machining only where a mating face needs it. The scale of the machine is a consequence of the part size, not a new process. And the trade-offs are the standard ones: a die that costs far more than a stamping die set for a single part, amortised over a very high volume; a casting alloy in place of wrought sheet; and repairability questions that follow from replacing a bolted and welded assembly with one casting.

The figures here come from Tesla's published letter and call, as reported by trade press; the course does not use the cost savings or robot counts that circulate without a Tesla document behind them.

Common mistakes

  1. Drawing a casting like a machined part. Consequence: sharp corners, no draft, and thick sections; the foundry's DFM review rewrites the part. Fix: fillets everywhere, draft on every surface perpendicular to the parting line, uniform walls.
  2. Tolerancing across the parting line. Consequence: parting-line shift consumes the tolerance and parts fail inspection. Fix: keep functional dimensions in one die half; machine the rest.
  3. Precision tolerances everywhere. Consequence: faster die wear, shorter runs between maintenance, higher price. Fix: standard tolerances by default; precision only where function requires, as NADCA advises.
  4. Machining deep into a die casting. Consequence: porosity exposed on a sealing face; leaks. Fix: thin machining stock on the dense skin, and sealing faces where fill is good.
  5. Same draft inside and outside. Consequence: castings stick to cores and distort on ejection. Fix: about twice the draft on inside surfaces.
  6. Sharp fillets on forgings. Consequence: incomplete fill, extra preform dies, die cracking. Fix: fillet radii at the FIA preferred values for the rib height and alloy.
  7. Datums on features from different die halves. Consequence: the machining fixture locates on surfaces that move relative to each other; scrap at first article. Fix: datum targets on one die half, with cast locating pads.
  8. Assuming the metal is sound. Consequence: internal defects reach the field. Fix: agree the inspection method (X-ray, ultrasonic, pressure test) and the acceptance criteria with the supplier before tooling, and design critical areas so they can be inspected.

Worked exercise: draft and tolerance on a die-cast bracket

This is an illustrative example with invented but realistic numbers, not a real program.

An aluminium die-cast bracket has a pocket 40 mm deep on the ejector side, formed by a core, and an outside wall of the same depth. A mounting face is 127 mm long and is formed entirely in the ejector half. A second dimension of 60 mm runs across the parting line to a feature in the cover half.

Tasks. (a) Compute the NADCA standard draft distance and angle for the inside wall of the pocket and for the outside wall. (b) Compare with the general note of 2° inside and 1° outside. (c) State the standard and precision linear tolerances for the 127 mm face. (d) Explain what happens to the 60 mm dimension and what you would do if it must be ±0.20 mm.

Show the worked solution

(a) Draft by formula. Inside wall, aluminium, C = 6.00 (metric): D = √40 / 6.00 = 6.32 / 6.00 = 1.05 mm over 40 mm; angle = arctan(1.05 / 40) ≈ 1.5°. Outside wall, C = 12.00: D = 6.32 / 12.00 = 0.53 mm; angle ≈ 0.75°. Check against NADCA's inch example: a 25.4 mm deep inside wall gives D = √25.4 / 6.00 = 0.84 mm, or 1.9°, which matches the standard's example of about 2° at one inch of depth.

(b) Versus the general note. The general note (2° inside, 1° outside for features at least 25 mm deep) is more conservative than the formula at 40 mm depth (1.5° and 0.75°), because the formula's angle falls with depth. Either is acceptable; the general note is simpler to put on a drawing, and the formula tells you how much you could reduce it if a specific feature needs less. Do not go below the formula without asking for precision tolerances.

(c) Linear tolerance on the 127 mm face. 127 mm is 5 in: the first 25.4 mm plus four additional 25.4 mm increments. Standard: ±0.25 + 4 × 0.025 = ±0.35 mm. Precision: ±0.05 + 4 × 0.025 = ±0.15 mm. These are NADCA's own worked values for a 5 in aluminium casting.

(d) The 60 mm dimension across the parting line. The linear tolerance tables do not apply to it; NADCA requires the tolerance to be increased for parting-line shift using its separate tables. If the function needs ±0.20 mm, the practical options are: move the feature so both ends are formed in the same die half; or machine one end after casting, which turns a cross-parting-line casting dimension into a machined dimension at about ±0.125 mm standard capability, with a cast-in locating pad in the ejector half as the datum for that machining setup.

Key takeaways

  • Die casting for high volume and thin walls, investment casting for complex shapes and hard alloys at lower volume, sand casting for size and cheap tooling, forging for fatigue and impact loads.
  • Draft is depth- and alloy-dependent; inside surfaces need about twice the draft of outside surfaces because the alloy shrinks onto cores. NADCA's general note for aluminium: 2° inside, 1° outside.
  • Casting tolerances apply only within one die half. Dimensions across the parting line or through slides carry extra variation; keep function in one half or machine it.
  • Standard tolerances by default. Precision tolerances shorten die life and runs between maintenance, so specify them only where function requires.
  • Forgings want generous fillets for metal flow, balanced loads to avoid counterlocks, and datums in one die half.
  • Leave thin machining stock only where a face must be machined, and locate the first machining setup on cast-in pads from one die half.
  • The process puts material where you cannot see it. Agree the inspection method and acceptance criteria before tooling; a defect in the melt is a defect in the part.
