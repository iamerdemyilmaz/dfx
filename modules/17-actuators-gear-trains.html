Module 17 · Part 8: Robotic systems

Actuators and gear trains: DFx and failure modes

A robot joint concentrates every problem in this course into one housing: a motor, a gear train, bearings with preload, an encoder, a brake, seals and grease, and a harness, all located by two bores whose alignment decides whether the assembly lasts a year or a month. Then the company has to build hundreds of them. This module reads the specification sheet the way a manufacturing engineer has to, names the failure modes by their standards, and turns both into design rules.

This module is written for a company building mobile or manipulating robots at fleet scale. No specific company's internal practice is described. Named products appear only where a published document says what is claimed.

Learning objectives

  • Choose between planetary, strain wave, cycloidal, and direct or quasi-direct drive for a stated torque, speed, backlash, and cost target, and defend the choice.
  • Read an actuator specification: rated, peak, and momentary peak torque; backlash and lost motion; torsional stiffness; efficiency; rated life.
  • Compute a bearing L10 life under a load spectrum and a bore-to-bore alignment stack-up.
  • Name gear and bearing failure modes in the terms of ANSI/AGMA 1010 and ISO 15243, and trace each to the design or process decision that causes it early.
  • Specify the manufacturing controls a joint needs: bore datum strategy, fit selection, press-fit monitoring, preload setting, grease dosing, encoder alignment, traceability.

Before you start

Three quick questions on what this module covers. They do not count toward completion; use them to see what you already know, then read on. The module quiz at the end is the one that marks the module complete.

1. Backlash in a gear train is

Backlash is read at zero torque on the hysteresis curve; lost motion is read at a small fraction of rated torque and includes elastic wind-up.

2. A bearing's L10 life is the life that

L10 is the 90 percent reliability life under ISO 281; it does not cover wear, corrosion, or electrical erosion.

3. Halving the load on a ball bearing changes its rating life by about

L10 = (C/P)^3 for ball bearings and (C/P)^(10/3) for roller bearings. Load, not speed, is the lever.

Why this matters

The International Space Station's starboard Solar Alpha Rotary Joint is a bearing the size of a room: a nitrided steel race ring loaded by twelve trundle bearing arms spaced at 30° intervals, turning the solar arrays to track the sun. In 2008 it failed. The investigation that followed used spacewalks to collect debris and samples, structural and thermal analysis, and a review of the build records. NASA's report gives the root cause as "kinematic design vulnerability coupled with inadequate lubrication, and manufacturing flaws", corroborated by ground tests, metallurgy, and modelling; the recovery replaced worn components in orbit and applied lubricant.[1] The gold film that was meant to transfer onto the race and lower friction had worn away on the failed joint and was still pristine on the port joint's spare rollers.[1]

Nothing in that sentence is exotic. A kinematic arrangement that loaded the contact in a way the design did not intend, a lubricant that could not keep up, and a manufacturing flaw: those are the three ways a robot actuator fails early on Earth as well, and they are decided before the first unit is built. The rest of the course taught the processes. This module puts them together in the one assembly a robotics company builds more of than anything else.

The joint as a stack of tolerances

Figure 1 is a generic rotary actuator: a frameless motor on a shaft, a reduction stage, an output bearing carrying the load, an input bearing, an encoder at each end, a brake, seals, and a housing. Every one of those parts is located by a bore or a face in the housing, and the housing is where the stack begins.

Three things follow. First, the datum strategy of Module 3 is not a drawing nicety here: the bearing bores must be toleranced to one axis, and the cheapest way to make them coaxial is to machine both in one setup, so that their relationship comes from the machine spindle rather than from two fixtures. Second, radial offsets add along the axis and divide by the bearing span to become a tilt; a tilt of a few tenths of a milliradian across a 20 mm gear face concentrates the load on one end of every tooth, which is the misalignment edge loading behind most early gear failures. Third, the encoder gap, the seal running surface, and the brake face all ride on the same stack, so a bore that is out of position shows up as an encoder fault, a leak, or a dragging brake before it shows up as a gear failure.

Reading an actuator specification

Gear manufacturers define their terms precisely, and the definitions differ in ways that matter to a test engineer. Nabtesco's catalogue reads stiffness and lost motion from a torsional hysteresis curve taken with the input fixed and torque applied at the output: lost motion is the torsion angle at the mid-point of the hysteresis width at ±3 percent of rated torque, and backlash is the torsion angle where the torque is zero.[3] Harmonic Drive defines lost motion as the rotation of the low-speed shaft when the high-speed shaft is fixed and a slight load torque is applied at the output in each direction.[2] The two numbers are not interchangeable, and a planetary gearbox's "torsional backlash" in arcminutes is a third convention. When you write an end-of-line test, you write down which one you are measuring and at what torque.

Torque ratings

A catalogue gives at least three torques. Rated torque is what the life figure is quoted at. A repeated peak or acceleration torque is allowed for a stated number of cycles. A momentary peak torque covers collisions, and it has a mechanical basis: Harmonic Drive notes that momentary peak torque can be limited by the flexspline's bolted joint rather than by the gear teeth, and that if excessive torque, which the catalogue puts at 8 to 9 times rated torque, is applied while the gear is in motion, the teeth may not engage properly. That event is called ratcheting, and it "may cause the Flexspline to become non-concentric with the Circular Spline", after which continued operation shortens life and can end in flexspline fatigue failure.[2] Nabtesco sets the momentary maximum allowable torque of its cycloidal reducers at 500 percent of rated and requires shock loads to be counted against a cycle limit.[3] For a robot that will be crashed into things, the number that matters is the one for the collision case, and it comes with a cycle budget.

Life

Gear life in a strain wave unit is the life of the wave generator bearing. Harmonic Drive quotes L10 of 7,000 hours for its CSF, CSD, SHF, SHD, and CSF-mini series and 10,000 hours for CSG and SHG, with L50 of 35,000 and 50,000 hours, at rated torque and rated input speed, and scales it with the formula Lh = Ln (Tr/Tav)3 (Nr/Nav) for the average load and speed of the duty cycle.[2] Nabtesco's rated service life is L10 of 6,000 hours at rated torque and speed.[3] The cube in that formula is the same cube that governs bearings, and it is the single most useful fact in actuator sizing: a duty cycle that averages 60 percent of rated torque gives about 4.6 times the rated life.

Backlash classes

Planetary suppliers sell backlash in grades. WITTENSTEIN's product lines run from 0 arcmin for its Galaxie drive, through ≤1 to 3 arcmin and ≤1 to 4 arcmin for the premium and advanced lines, to ≤5, ≤8, and ≤12 arcmin for the value and basic lines.[4] Neugart offers reduced backlash under 1 arcmin on selected frame sizes.[5] Each step down in backlash is a step up in gear accuracy class, selective assembly, or preloading, and therefore in price.

The quasi-direct drive case is worth reading in the original papers because it inverts the usual instinct. Wensing and colleagues at MIT argued that conventional manufacturing-robot actuators cannot deliver the high torque density and impact handling that dynamic legged machines need, and proposed proprioceptive actuation: collocated force control at the joint through a low-ratio, backdrivable transmission, with an impact mitigation factor to quantify backdrivability; the Cheetah leg controlled contact forces at contact times down to 85 ms and peak forces over 450 N.[6] Grimminger and colleagues built an open-source module from a brushless motor, a 9:1 dual-stage timing belt transmission, and an optical encoder on the motor shaft; it weighs 150 g and eight of them make a 2.2 kg quadruped, and the low ratio gives enough transparency to control torque from motor current alone.[7] Katz's thesis actuator uses a drone-class motor with a 6:1 single-stage planetary reduction and an integrated controller; its bill of materials is about US$300 in sub-50 quantities, one to two orders of magnitude below the Cheetah's actuators, and it accepts about 0.005 rad (0.28°) of output backlash from off-the-shelf gears because positioning accuracy and backlash were deliberately not design drivers.[8]

The manufacturing lesson is that architecture decides the factory. A strain wave joint is bought as a component and its factory is the supplier's; the buyer's job is the housing, the bolted joints, the bearing preload, and the test. A quasi-direct drive joint is built from commodity parts and its factory is yours: gear inspection, press fits, encoder alignment, and the calibration that makes torque from current honest.

Gear failure modes, by their names

ANSI/AGMA 1010 is the standard nomenclature for gear tooth wear and failure; it classifies and describes the common modes so that an engineer looking at a failed tooth can name what happened and judge how far it has progressed.[10] Its families, as used in this course:

  • Pitting and micropitting. Surface fatigue from repeated contact stress; micropitting as a grey frosting on the flank, macropitting as craters. Driven by load, lubricant film, and surface finish.
  • Scuffing. Adhesive welding and tearing of the flanks when the lubricant film fails, usually at high sliding speed and temperature. Sudden, not fatigue.
  • Wear. Abrasive or adhesive removal of material; the profile changes and backlash grows.
  • Tooth root bending fatigue. Cracks at the root fillet that end in breakage; the mode that a rating method such as ISO 6336 is designed to prevent.[26]
  • Misalignment edge loading. Not a mode but a cause: a tilt between mating gears puts the whole load on one end of the face, and every mode above arrives early. In a robot joint the tilt comes from the housing stack of Figure 1.

The accuracy that resists these is specified by flank tolerance class under ISO 1328-1, which defines the terms, the class structure, and allowable values for cylindrical involute gears.[9] Commonly quoted process capabilities put hobbing at roughly AGMA quality 8 to 11, shaping and shaving near 9 to 10, honing at 10 to 13, and grinding at 12 and above, with heat treatment after cutting costing about a class; these are vendor figures and vary with machine, tooling, and material, so specify the class from the standard and ask the gear maker which process reaches it.[25]

Bearing failure modes and bearing life

ISO 15243 classifies in-service failures of rolling bearings by appearance and cause.[13] SKF's failure atlas lays the standard's tree out as six modes with sub-modes: rolling contact fatigue (subsurface initiated, surface initiated); wear (abrasive, adhesive); corrosion (moisture corrosion, and frictional corrosion divided into fretting corrosion and false brinelling); electrical erosion (excessive current, current leakage); plastic deformation (overload, indentations from particles); and cracking and fracture.[14] Three of these are the robot engineer's daily concern:

  • False brinelling is frictional corrosion at rolling element spacing whose root cause is vibration during standstill; SKF's example is a bearing in auxiliary equipment with long standstill periods, showing several sets of marks, one per standstill.[14] A robot that ships by truck with its joints unpowered, or that idles on a vibrating floor, is that bearing.
  • True brinelling is plastic deformation from overload or shock: a dropped joint, an impact, a press-fit force taken through the rolling elements.[14]
  • Preload loss is wear's consequence. NASA's life tests of the grease-lubricated input-shaft bearings of the Space Shuttle body flap actuators found "wear that can cause loss of bearing preload" as the failure mechanism, and used a two-parameter Weibull analysis of the rig data to put reliability at 96.9 percent for 12 missions for the four actuators on one vehicle.[16] A preloaded pair that loses preload becomes a pair with clearance, and the encoder sees it first.

Life

ISO 281 defines the basic rating life as the life associated with 90 percent reliability under conventional conditions and adds a modified life for other reliabilities, lubrication, and contamination; it explicitly does not cover wear, corrosion, or electrical erosion.[11] The working formula, in NSK's words, is that L10 is how long identical bearings run until flaking appears on 10 percent of them, with L10 = (C/P)3 for ball bearings and (C/P)10/3 for roller bearings, in millions of revolutions, where C is the dynamic load rating and P the equivalent load; halving the load gives about 8 times the life for a ball bearing and 10 times for a roller bearing.[12] Two consequences for actuators. Life is a load problem far more than a speed problem, so the output bearing's moment load from the arm, not the motor speed, sizes it. And an L10 says nothing about the failures a robot actually sees, which are mostly the excluded ones: contamination, false brinelling, preload loss, and lubricant death.

NASA's mechanisms lessons-learned study, a review of decades of flight anomalies, is blunt on the fundamentals: use rolling rather than sliding contact wherever possible, lubricate or separate every moving surface with a suitable grease or dry film "no exceptions", keep loads on hard coatings below the substrate's bearing yield strength, and use a compliant preload where thermal cycling would otherwise brinell the races.[15] Those were written for spacecraft, and every one of them applies to a joint in a warehouse.

Strain wave, cycloidal, planetary: what wears

Strain wave. The flexspline is a thin steel cup flexed twice per input revolution; it is a fatigue part by design and its life is set by the wave generator bearing, as the catalogue life figures show.[2] Ratcheting under overload leaves it non-concentric and starts a fatigue failure.[2] Lost motion grows over life as flank wear and wave generator bearing wear accumulate, so an end-of-line lost motion value is a baseline, not a constant. Ratcheting torque depends on the stiffness of the housing the circular spline is bolted into, which is a housing design decision, not a supplier one.[2]

Cycloidal. Load is shared between pins and rollers and disks on eccentric crankshafts; wear of pins and rollers and misalignment of the carrier are the life items, and the integrated main bearing carries the arm's moment, so the moment rating is as important as the torque rating.[3]

Planetary. Load sharing between planets depends on carrier pin position accuracy and ring bore concentricity; a carrier that is out puts more than its share on one planet and that planet pits first. Backlash grows with flank wear; a run-in period is normal and should be part of the test plan rather than a surprise.

Lubrication. Klüber's rolling bearing guidance selects grease by operating temperature, speed factor, load ratio C/P, consistency, and base oil viscosity, and warns that the grease's upper service temperature should exceed the bearing's operating temperature by a generous margin, because above it the grease ages quickly and relubrication becomes necessary where lubrication for life was intended.[18] For a sealed actuator the design questions are fill quantity (too much churns and heats, too little starves), leakage paths past shaft seals, and the temperature the grease sees next to a motor winding.

Motors, encoders, brakes, fasteners, wheels, frames, batteries

Motor and electronics failures the mechanical engineer owns. Winding over-temperature is a thermal path problem: the stator sits in the housing and the housing is the heat sink. Magnet demagnetisation follows over-temperature. Encoder faults are contamination, air gap, and alignment: a magnetic or optical encoder needs a gap and a concentricity held by the same bores as everything else, and field-oriented control needs the electrical offset between the rotor's d-axis and the encoder's index, found at calibration by aligning the rotor with a d-axis excitation and reading the encoder; that offset is a per-unit number and belongs in the unit's record.[23] Cable fatigue at moving joints and connector wear are the harness items of Module 20.

Brakes. A friction brake wears, glazes, and drifts in holding torque; a spring-applied brake that must release before motion is a mechanism that can stick. The Curiosity rover's drill feed stalled shortly after disengaging its magnetically actuated friction brake at the start of a move, and the actuator then moved in and out of a high-drag state until the mechanism could no longer be relied on.[17] Holding torque and release are both end-of-line test items.

Fasteners under vibration. The Junker test applies transverse displacement to a bolted joint and plots preload against cycles; an unsecured joint is first run at increasing displacement until it loosens in about 300 cycles, and the secured joint is then run at that displacement for at least 2,000 cycles to show what preload remains.[19] ISO 16130 standardises the method for aerospace fasteners as a comparative evaluation of locking elements, and says explicitly that the result is not an absolute statement of service behaviour because rig stiffness differs.[20] In a robot the practical controls are the ones of Module 13: torque control, thread locker where disassembly allows, and designing joints so that transverse motion cannot occur.

Wheels and tread. Polyurethane treads are specified by hardness and chemistry. Blickle's standard treads are 92 ± 3 Shore A for its Extrathane and Besthane materials and 75 Shore A for its soft grades; the Besthane grades are hydrolysis resistant and resist heat build-up under dynamic load, and are rated for speeds up to 16 km/h.[21] Flat spotting under sustained static load, delamination from the hub, and wear rate against the floor are the field items; a fleet robot that parks loaded for a weekend tests the first of these every week.

Frames, welds, and batteries. Brackets fail at welds by fatigue under vibration, which is Module 13's welding section plus a vibration test. Battery packs and charging contacts are mechanical wear items too: a contact that mates thousands of times a year needs a mating cycle rating, and a pack that is dropped needs the abuse tests of Module 20.

Grid and drive-unit mechanisms in public documents. Two public records show what fleet-scale mechanisms look like. The Kiva Systems patent describes a mobile drive unit with a docking head, including a self-aligning docking cone, raised by a docking actuator to lift an inventory holder, a motorised axle with two driven wheels and two stabilising wheels, and a position sensor reading reference markers on the floor.[27] The 2023 English Patents Court judgment in AutoStore v Ocado describes grid storage robots that run on rails in X and Y, raising one wheel set while the other drives, lift bins with a gripper, and differ in whether the bin is carried in a cantilevered position or a central cavity, with the footprint consequences that follow.[28] Wheel change, lifting, and charging mechanisms of that kind are the drive-unit actuators this module is about.

Real-world example: a brake, a bearing, and a drill

Two NASA cases bracket what goes wrong in a small actuator. On 30 November 2016, Sol 1536 of the mission, Curiosity was commanded to drill its sixteenth rock sample. The drill feed mechanism stalled shortly after disengaging its magnetically actuated friction brake at the start of the extension move. Diagnostic experiments showed the actuator moving in and out of a high-drag state in which it could not actuate; reliability degraded over the following months, and by June 2017 the team concluded the feed could not be relied on. They succeeded in fully extending the feed and developed a new sampling technique that used the arm instead of the feed, collecting the first feed-extended sample on Sol 2057, 521 sols after the stall.[17] The JPL paper on the anomaly draws lessons on mechanism design, mission architecture, and operations; for a fleet engineer the transferable one is that a brake is a mechanism with its own failure modes, and that a drag state that comes and goes is exactly what an end-of-line no-load torque sweep exists to catch before shipment.

The second case is slower. The Shuttle's body flap actuators had grease-lubricated ball bearings on their input shafts. Rig tests to failure found wear that caused loss of preload, and a Weibull analysis of the test data gave a reliability of 96.9 percent for 12 missions for the four actuators on a vehicle, each with a two-bearing shaft.[16] That is a preloaded pair losing its preload through wear rather than fatigue, which is the life the L10 formula does not predict, found by testing to failure rather than by calculation. The fleet equivalent is a sample of actuators run to failure at the qualification build, so that the life the field will see is measured, not assumed.

Common mistakes

  1. Sizing the gear on peak torque and the bearing on speed. Consequence: an oversized gearbox with an undersized output bearing that fails under the arm's moment. Fix: size the gear on the duty cycle average with the cubic life formula and the bearing on the equivalent load.
  2. Two setups for two bores. Consequence: a coaxiality that depends on two fixtures, a tilt across the gear face, and pitting on one end of every tooth. Fix: one datum axis, one setup, and the stack-up on the drawing.
  3. Pressing bearings through the rolling elements. Consequence: true brinelling at assembly, found as noise at end of line or as spalling in the field. Fix: press on the ring being fitted, monitor force versus displacement.
  4. Treating preload as a dimension. Consequence: a shim thickness on the drawing and preload that varies with every stack. Fix: a setting method chosen for the volume, with the measurement recorded per unit.
  5. Shipping and idling unpowered on vibration. Consequence: false brinelling before the robot has worked a day. Fix: transport fixtures that unload the joints or lock them, and idle strategies that move them.
  6. Ignoring the collision case. Consequence: a ratcheted flexspline and a fatigue failure weeks later. Fix: specify momentary peak torque for the impact the robot will actually see, with a cycle budget, and log overloads.
  7. Grease chosen by name. Consequence: a grease near its upper service temperature next to a motor winding, aged in months. Fix: select by temperature margin, speed factor, and load ratio, and measure the temperature at the bearing in DVT.
  8. Backlash bought instead of designed. Consequence: a premium backlash class paid for on a joint whose function never needed it. Fix: write down the positioning requirement, then buy the class it implies.

Worked exercise: pick the actuator, size the bearing, check the bores

This is an illustrative example with invented but realistic numbers, not a real program. Catalogue values named in the solution are the ones cited above; everything else is an assumption stated in the text.

A mobile manipulator's shoulder joint needs 60 N·m continuous and 150 N·m for collisions, at 30 rpm output, with lost motion no more than 3 arcmin, inside a 90 mm diameter envelope, for 20,000 units per year with a target of 40,000 hours of life at a duty cycle averaging 60 percent of rated torque.

  1. Architecture. Compare a 6:1 quasi-direct drive, a 10:1 planetary, and a 50:1 strain wave on motor torque, backlash, collision handling, and factory implications, and choose.
  2. Output bearing life. The output cross-roller bearing has a dynamic load rating C of 28 kN. The radial-equivalent load spectrum is 2.5 kN for 55 percent of the time, 5.0 kN for 35 percent, and 9.0 kN for 10 percent. Compute L10 in hours at 30 rpm, and compare with sizing on the 9.0 kN peak alone.
  3. Bore-to-bore stack-up. The two bearing bores are 70 mm apart. Contributors, as radial offsets: bore A position to datum 0.010 mm, bore B position to datum 0.010 mm, outer ring runout 0.005 mm at each bearing, radial clearance 0.004 mm at each bearing, shaft journal eccentricity 0.006 mm. Compute the worst-case and RSS tilt and the resulting offset across a 20 mm gear face. Then recompute the worst case if both bores are machined in one setup with a direct coaxiality of 0.008 mm.
Show the worked solution

1. Architecture. Motor torque needed: at 6:1, 10 N·m continuous and 25 N·m peak at 180 rpm; at 10:1, 6 and 15 N·m at 300 rpm; at 50:1, 1.2 and 3 N·m at 1,500 rpm. The quasi-direct drive needs a large, expensive motor and accepts backlash of the order of Katz's 0.005 rad (17 arcmin), which fails the 3 arcmin lost motion requirement outright.[8] The 10:1 planetary meets 3 arcmin only in the premium classes (≤1 to 3 arcmin), which is the price step the requirement forces.[4] The 50:1 strain wave meets lost motion easily and fits the envelope with the smallest motor; its exposures are torsional compliance and the collision case: 150 N·m against a unit rated near 60 N·m is 2.5 times rated, well inside the momentary peak rating and far below the 8 to 9 times rated at which the catalogue warns of ratcheting, provided the housing stiffness the supplier assumes is met.[2] Choice: strain wave, with the housing stiffness and bolted joint designed to the supplier's data, a cycle budget for collisions, and lost motion measured at end of line as the baseline for growth. The factory consequence is that the gear is bought and the housing, bearings, and test are yours.

2. Output bearing life. For a roller bearing, p = 10/3. The equivalent load for a stepped spectrum is P = (Σ qi Pip)1/p = (0.55 × 2.53.333 + 0.35 × 5.03.333 + 0.10 × 9.03.333)0.3 = 5.17 kN. L10 = (28 ÷ 5.17)3.333 = 280 million revolutions. At 30 rpm that is 280 × 106 ÷ (60 × 30) = 155,500 hours, about 39 years at 4,000 hours a year: the bearing is not the life limit. Sized on the 9.0 kN peak alone, L10 would be (28 ÷ 9)3.333 × 106 ÷ 1,800 = 24,400 hours, which would fail the 40,000 hour target and lead to a needlessly larger bearing. The spectrum matters. Remember what this number excludes: false brinelling in transport, contamination past the seal, and preload loss through wear are not in it.[11][12]

3. Stack-up. Worst case: 0.010 + 0.010 + 0.005 + 0.005 + 0.004 + 0.004 + 0.006 = 0.044 mm radial offset over 70 mm, a tilt of 0.63 mrad (0.036°), which across a 20 mm gear face is 12.6 µm of offset from one end of the tooth to the other. RSS: √(0.010² + 0.010² + 0.005² + 0.005² + 0.004² + 0.004² + 0.006²) = 0.018 mm, a tilt of 0.25 mrad and 5 µm across the face. Machining both bores in one setup replaces the two 0.010 mm position terms with one 0.008 mm coaxiality: worst case 0.032 mm and 0.46 mrad, a 27 percent reduction from a process decision that costs nothing at the drawing. Whether 12.6 µm across a 20 mm face is acceptable depends on the gear class and the crowning the supplier applies; the exercise's point is that the housing decides the number, and Module 3's method puts it on the drawing.

Proprioceptive actuation trades backlash and torque density for backdrivability and cost.

Answer key
  1. c. Mid-point width at ±3 percent of rated torque.
  2. About 64,800 hours.
  3. b. Teeth failing to engage under excessive torque in motion.
  4. About 10.1.
  5. a. Standstill vibration.
  6. d. Wear and preload loss.
  7. b. Coaxiality from the machine, not the fixtures.
  8. c. Force control and impact survival.

Key takeaways

  • A joint is a stack: two bearing bores, their fits and preload, the gear centre distance, and the encoder gap. Tolerance them to one axis and machine them in one setup.
  • Read the specification in the supplier's own definitions: backlash at zero torque, lost motion at a stated torque fraction, momentary peak with a cycle budget, life at rated conditions scaled by the cube of the torque ratio.
  • Architecture decides the factory. Strain wave and cycloidal units are bought; quasi-direct drives are built from commodity parts and calibrated.
  • Name failures by their standards: AGMA 1010 for gears, ISO 15243 for bearings. Misalignment brings every mode early.
  • L10 is a fatigue life at 90 percent reliability. Robots mostly die of what it excludes: false brinelling, contamination, preload loss, dead grease. Test to failure to find those.
  • Brakes, fasteners, treads, encoders, and grease are mechanisms with their own failure modes and their own end-of-line tests.
  • The fundamentals of the space mechanisms lessons apply on the warehouse floor: lubricate or separate every moving surface, no exceptions.
